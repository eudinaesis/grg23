Teacher notes

Learning goals

We begin with familiar goods, then trace the systems behind them. The goal is not to claim that everything is connected to everything. It is to find specific links, name the mechanism, and ask who has choices and who bears costs.

  • Trace at least three steps between a global event and a price or supply change in Austria.
  • Distinguish a documented link from a plausible but unverified one.
  • Explain how one disruption can affect groups differently.

Your 100-minute lesson

  1. 10 min

    Opening: goods and connections

    Read the case reader ‘A basket has a backstory’. Individually choose one item in the ‘Basket cards’ table and write one question about where it comes from or what it depends on.

  2. 15 min

    Build a first map

    Use ‘Supply-chain cards’ to arrange a chain from event to effect. Draw arrows only when your group can write a reason beside each arrow. Submit one annotated draft map.

  3. 15 min

    Source check

    Read ‘Evidence cards’. Label each claim documented, plausible, or unsupported from the card. Add one evidence note to your map.

  4. 20 min

    Case teams

    Use ‘Supply-chain cards’ and ‘Impact table’. For your assigned item, explain who may face higher costs, delay, or reduced choice. Complete the table without using personal household spending information.

  5. 5 min

    Break

    Take a five-minute break.

  6. 20 min

    Map exchange

    Exchange maps with another group. Use the ‘Impact table and map-review checklist’ to identify a missing mechanism, a strong evidence link, and one uncertainty. Return the reviewed map.

  7. 10 min

    Whole-class synthesis

    Add one well-supported link and one uncertainty to the class map. In the ‘Impact table and map-review checklist (Connection ledger)’, record one possible connection that needs more evidence.

  8. 5 min

    Individual close

    Complete the two exit questions. In your notebook, sketch your individual map and write a one-sentence provisional claim for homework.

Materials

Case reader: A basket has a backstory

A basket has a backstory

A loaf of bread, a winter jacket, and a phone look like separate products. Each depends on networks of people, materials, energy, transport, rules, and money. Wheat may be grown in one region, milled in another, and baked near the customer. A jacket can contain cotton or synthetic fibres, be sewn far from where it is designed, and travel through ports and distribution centres. A phone combines mined minerals, refined materials, specialist components, software, assembly, and repair networks.

A supply chain is the set of steps and relationships that bring a product to a user. A disruption can travel along it, but the effect is not automatic. A drought may reduce a crop; a smaller harvest may raise a commodity price; a mill or food company may absorb some of the increase, pass some on, or change suppliers. The final price also depends on energy, wages, transport, taxes, contracts, competition, and decisions by firms. The chain therefore needs evidence at each step.

Some links are physical: a factory needs electricity or a ship carries goods. Others are financial or political: a company borrows money, a government sets a tariff, or a conflict changes access to a route. A chain can also have alternatives. A buyer may switch suppliers, use stored materials, redesign a product, or accept a delay. How quickly it can adapt depends on power and resources.

Effects are unequal. A large company may negotiate long contracts; a small shop may have less bargaining power. A price increase matters more to a household that spends a larger share of its income on essentials. Workers may face overtime, reduced hours, or job changes. A country that imports a key input may be more exposed than one that produces it. These are questions to investigate, not conclusions to assume.

Polycrisis analysis asks whether several disruptions interact so that their combined effects are more serious or harder to manage. Today we practise the first step: make a careful, limited map. A useful map says what is known, what is a reasoned possibility, and what evidence would help decide.

Evidence cards

Evidence practice
CardStatementHow to use it
1Eurostat reports that EU net imports supplied 62.5% of gross available energy in 2022.Documented statistic for the EU overall; it does not identify the source of every product’s price change.
2“If gas gets more expensive, every loaf of bread costs exactly 20 cents more.”Unsupported as written: it gives no date, bakery, cost data, or pass-through calculation.
3“A phone assembly delay could delay deliveries to a shop.”Plausible mechanism; investigate the product and event before stating it occurred.
4“A global price increase affects every household equally.”Overgeneralisation; test with income, consumption, contract, and substitution differences.

Map review: Does every arrow have a mechanism? Are dates and places clear? Is a fact separated from an interpretation? Have you shown who may gain as well as who may lose? Have you marked alternatives and uncertainty?

Connection ledger: Claim / current label (documented, plausible, unknown) / evidence needed.

Language bridge

English pathway: Discuss and write in English. Austrian connection: choose one product sold in Austria and identify a local stage (shop, bakery, repair service, transport, or public rule) as well as an international stage. Original-reading extension: In Eurostat’s Key figures on Europe 2024, read the paragraph and chart section headed “Energy dependency rate” (PDF, search within the document for that exact heading). Explain what the 62.5% EU figure measures and name one thing it does not tell us.

Key terms: supply chain = the steps and relationships bringing a product to users; input = material or service used to make something; pass-through = how much of a cost change is reflected in a later price; exposure = how strongly someone may be affected.

What to hand in

Individual, final 250–350 words plus a hand-drawn or digital annotated map; begin with a short individual outline in class, then finish the response as homework using your group map. Explain one product chain in at least four steps, label each arrow with its mechanism, cite one supplied evidence card, identify one unequal effect, and mark one uncertain link. Submit through the teacher’s assignment platform or on paper. Success: mechanism is clear; evidence is used within its limits; uncertainty is visible.

Write on paper or use the assignment platform your teacher names. This website does not collect responses.

Exit ticket

  • Which arrow on your map has the strongest evidence, and why?
  • What additional fact would most improve your explanation?

After class · up to 25 minutes

Spend up to 20 minutes finishing the individual response from your class outline and finalising the annotated map. Add a small ‘what I still need to know’ box with two specific questions. Use only the supplied cards; do not research a brand or disclose family spending.
