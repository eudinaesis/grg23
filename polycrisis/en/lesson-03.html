Teacher notes

Learning goals

Europe’s energy shock had several causes and consequences. We examine the period through dated evidence, keeping physical supply, market prices, national choices, and household effects distinct. The aim is to reconstruct mechanisms, not to tell a one-cause story.

  • Build a dated sequence of energy-market developments in Europe from 2021 to 2023.
  • Explain how import dependence, supply changes, prices, and policy responses can interact.
  • Compare who had room to respond and identify what the evidence cannot establish.

Your 100-minute lesson

  1. 10 min

    Read the case reader

    Read ‘Gas, electricity, and choices’. Underline one dated event, circle one mechanism, and put a question mark beside a claim needing evidence.

  2. 15 min

    Build the timeline

    Use ‘Timeline evidence cards’ to arrange events from 2021 to 2023. Add a short note on what changed and what the card does not prove.

  3. 20 min

    Trace the electricity price mechanism

    Use the table in ‘How wholesale electricity pricing works’. Work through the fictional calculation; label it as a model, not a historical price series. Submit the completed table.

  4. 5 min

    Break

    Take a five-minute break.

  5. 20 min

    Policy response comparison

    Read ‘Response cards and who-can-act matrix’ and compare demand reduction, new supply, and household support. Complete the ‘Who can act?’ matrix with a benefit, cost, and unresolved question for each.

  6. 15 min

    Crisis map and challenge

    Groups connect one energy-supply change to one market effect and one distributional or policy effect. Peer-review using ‘Response cards and who-can-act matrix’ (Causal map check); add at least one alternative explanation or buffer.

  7. 10 min

    Individual paragraph

    Write a 150–200-word draft explanation: why was this more than a simple shortage? Use two dated cards and distinguish fact from interpretation.

  8. 5 min

    Exit

    Answer the two exit questions.

Materials

Case reader: Gas, electricity, and choices

Gas, electricity, and choices

Europe entered 2021 already facing a tight energy market. As economies reopened after COVID-19 restrictions, demand for energy increased. Gas storage and supply conditions varied between countries, and gas prices were rising before Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. The invasion and the reduction of Russian pipeline deliveries intensified uncertainty and changed the political setting. These facts matter because they prevent a simple story in which every price movement has one cause.

The European Union depended on imports for much of its energy. Eurostat reports that net imports accounted for 62.5% of EU gross available energy in 2022. Natural gas was one part of this wider energy system, used for heating, industry, and electricity generation. In 2021, Russia supplied 44% of EU natural-gas imports according to Eurostat’s 2023 energy overview. The figure describes the EU aggregate and a specific year; individual countries had different routes and dependencies.

Gas is not the only source of electricity, but gas-fired power plants can affect wholesale electricity prices. In many market hours, generators are dispatched from lower to higher operating cost to meet demand. In a simplified merit-order market, the last plant needed to meet demand can set the wholesale price received by generators. When gas is the marginal plant, a higher gas cost can raise the clearing price even for electricity produced by other plants. Actual market design, contracts, network limits, and hour-by-hour conditions are more complex than this classroom model.

Governments and the EU responded in several ways: seeking alternative supplies, expanding infrastructure and renewables, asking for lower gas demand, coordinating storage, and supporting consumers or firms. These measures addressed different problems and had different costs. More imports can improve short-term availability but create new supplier relationships. Price support can protect consumers quickly but costs public money and may weaken incentives to reduce use if poorly designed. Demand reduction can ease pressure but is harder for people with few alternatives.

The consequences were uneven across countries, sectors, firms, and households. A country’s import routes and energy mix mattered; so did contracts, income, building quality, and government support. One should not infer from a European average what every person experienced. Nor should a timeline alone prove that a policy caused a later price change. Our task is to connect the evidence carefully and ask where a response changed the chain, who benefited, and who carried the cost.

How wholesale electricity pricing works

Classroom model: fictional offer prices in euros per megawatt-hour (€/MWh). These are not historical prices. Demand requires 3 units of electricity. Dispatch the cheapest available units until demand is met. In this simplified model, the price of the last unit dispatched is paid for all units.

Language bridge

English pathway: Add Austria to the comparison: distinguish the EU aggregate from Austria’s import routes and household experience. Original-reading extension: In Eurostat’s Energy 2023 interactive publication, read the section “Where does our energy come from?” and its natural-gas import chart. State the year, denominator, and one limitation of the 44% figure.

Key terms: import dependency = reliance on net imports; wholesale = large-scale market before retail; marginal plant = last generator needed to meet demand in the simplified model; LNG = liquefied natural gas.

Exit ticket

  • Why is it misleading to say that the 2022 invasion explains every rise in energy prices?
  • Name one policy response and a group that might benefit or bear a cost.

After class · up to 25 minutes

Spend up to 25 minutes completing your 150–200-word explanation from the class draft and checking the dispatch calculation. Use at least two dated timeline cards; add one question you would ask before saying a policy caused a price change.
