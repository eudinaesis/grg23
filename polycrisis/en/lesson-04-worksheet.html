Your 100-minute lesson

  1. 10 min · Price or rate?

    Read ‘A number about many prices’. Complete ‘Quick check: level, change, or inflation’ with a partner. Give one worked calculation.

  2. 15 min · Build an index

    Use the fictional ‘Three-item basket’ table. Calculate basket cost in both periods, the percentage change, and each item’s contribution. Show your arithmetic.

  3. 15 min · Weighting and lived differences

    Compare the ‘Two fictional households’ baskets with the headline basket. Complete the ‘What the average hides’ table; do not use anyone’s real household budget.

  4. 5 min · Break

    Take a five-minute break.

  5. 20 min · Read Austria’s official number

    Use ‘Austria evidence card and source comparison’. Identify measure, period, number, unit, and whether it is an annual average or a December year-on-year rate. Write a careful headline.

  6. 15 min · Policy and distribution

    Use ‘Response choices’ to compare temporary support, indexation, and targeted transfers. For each, state who may benefit, one limitation, and what evidence would guide the choice.

  7. 15 min · Individual explanation

    Write 180–250 words explaining why the official Austrian inflation rate and a person’s experience can differ. Include the supplied statistic, a calculation or basket example, and one limitation.

  8. 5 min · Exit

    Answer both questions individually.

Materials

Case reader: A number about many prices

A number about many prices

Inflation is the rate at which a broad price index changes over time. If an index rises from 100 to 108.6 over a year, the increase is 8.6%. The price level is the index itself; the inflation rate is its percentage change over a stated period. If inflation falls from 8% to 3%, prices are still rising, but more slowly. A falling inflation rate is not automatically a fall in prices.

Statistical offices build consumer price indices by following prices for a basket of goods and services. The basket represents average spending patterns, and categories receive weights. A price change for an item with a larger weight can matter more to the overall index than the same percentage change for a rarely purchased item. Weights are updated to reflect spending patterns. The index is useful for tracking broad changes, comparing time periods, and informing policy; it is not a receipt for every household.

Households buy different things and face different prices. One household may heat with gas; another may use district heating. Rent, commuting, food choices, household size, contracts, and the timing of purchases vary. A student’s personal experience may be real but cannot by itself establish the national inflation rate. Conversely, an average index does not make a household’s experience unreal. The measures answer different questions.

For Austria, Statistik Austria reported an average annual inflation rate of 8.6% in 2022, compared with 2.8% in 2021, using the Austrian Consumer Price Index (VPI). Eurostat’s harmonised measure gave Austria a 10.5% year-on-year rate in December 2022. These numbers differ because one is the average change across the calendar year under the national index, while the other compares December 2022 with December 2021 using the harmonised index. They are not competing answers to exactly the same question.

Inflation also has distributional effects. A rise in essential prices can use a larger share of a lower-income household’s budget. Wages, pensions, savings, contracts, and public benefits may adjust at different times and by different rules. A policy that lowers a price for everyone can be quick but expensive; targeted support may focus resources but can miss people or arrive later. Statistics describe patterns; choices about what to do involve evidence and values.

Quick check: level, change, or inflation

Use the formula: percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100.

Response choices

Evaluate the instrument, not the political label.
Policy optionPossible benefitQuestion or limitation
Temporary broad price supportCan lower bills quickly for many users.How is it paid for? Does it also subsidise high consumption or higher-income users?
Targeted cash transferCan direct help to people with fewer resources.Who qualifies? Could eligible people be missed or receive help late?
Wage or benefit indexationCan help incomes catch up with price changes.Which index and time lag? How do different workers or contracts compare?
Energy efficiency investmentCan reduce future energy use and exposure.Who can afford upgrades? How quickly do savings arrive?

For each option, name likely beneficiaries, possible costs, the decision-maker, and one piece of evidence needed. Policy evaluation includes empirical questions and value choices.

Language bridge

English pathway: Explain the Austrian connection by comparing the Statistik Austria VPI annual average with the Eurostat HICP December rate. Original-reading extension: In the Statistik Austria 2022 annual release, read the opening paragraph under “Inflation im Jahr 2022 auf 8,6 % gestiegen” (the first paragraph of the release). Identify its index, period, figure, and comparator; then compare those with Eurostat’s December HICP table.

Key terms: price level = level of an index; inflation rate = percentage change over a stated period; weight = importance assigned to a category in the basket; HICP = EU-harmonised consumer price index; VPI = Austrian Consumer Price Index.

What to hand in

Individual explanation, final 180–250 words; draft in class and finish or revise as homework. Explain why Austria’s official 2022 rate and one person’s experience can differ. Include the 8.6% figure with correct measure and period, one calculation or basket example, and one limitation. Submit on paper or through the teacher’s assignment platform. Success: correct distinction between index and rate; weights explained; no claim that everyone experienced the average.
