Materials

Case reader: Sri Lanka—several causes, one crisis

Case reader: Sri Lanka—several causes, one crisis

Sri Lanka's economic crisis reached a dramatic point in 2022, when shortages of fuel, food and other imports, high inflation, power cuts and political upheaval affected daily life. No single event explains the outcome. A useful reconstruction separates long-standing vulnerabilities, outside shocks, decisions by national leaders, and the ways these forces travelled through the economy.

Before the pandemic, the government had already cut several taxes in late 2019. In its February 2022 assessment, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) said that the tax cuts and the pandemic contributed to large fiscal deficits in 2020 and 2021. Tourism had been hit after the 2019 Easter attacks and then by COVID-19. Tourism receipts are foreign currency income; fewer visitors can mean fewer dollars available for imports and debt payments. Remittances and exports are other sources of foreign currency, but they do not necessarily replace lost tourism quickly.

There were also domestic choices and vulnerabilities. The 2023 IMF programme report described low revenue, monetary financing of deficits, an effectively fixed exchange rate from April 2021, continued repayment of external debt, and a poorly timed ban on chemical fertiliser as part of the crisis context. These were decisions made under political and economic pressures, but they were not simply natural disasters. They can be investigated: what goal did a policy claim to serve, what mechanism did it use, and what consequences were foreseeable?

Then global conditions made the situation harder. The war in Ukraine contributed to higher world prices for fuel and food in 2022. Sri Lanka needed foreign currency to buy imports while its reserves were already low and borrowing access was constrained. Defending an exchange rate can use reserves; if reserves fall, importers may struggle to obtain dollars. A depreciating currency makes imported goods more expensive in local money and can raise the local-currency burden of foreign-currency debt. In April 2022, Sri Lanka announced a suspension of external debt service.

Sources emphasise different parts of this story. IMF staff reports combine data with the institution's analysis and policy recommendations. A statement by Sri Lanka's IMF executive director accepts that shocks mattered but gives stronger emphasis to policy missteps. Those perspectives should be compared, not collapsed into a single unquestioned account. This class uses them to build a causal explanation and to ask which links are well supported, which are interpretations, and what further evidence would help.

Source cards: evidence and perspective

Source card A — IMF staff, February 2022 (before the acute crisis)

The IMF's 2021 Article IV staff report said pre-pandemic tax cuts and COVID-19 contributed to fiscal deficits above 10% of GDP in 2020 and 2021, and public debt rose to 119% of GDP in 2021. It also described critically low reserves, lost access to international capital markets, external debt repayments, and foreign-exchange shortages. This is an institutional analysis published before the full crisis; it identifies vulnerabilities and risks, not a complete retrospective verdict.

Source card B — IMF staff, March 2023 (retrospective programme report)

The IMF report says Sri Lanka entered the pandemic with a thin reserve buffer, high debt and little fiscal space. It describes reduced tourism and foreign-exchange income, continued external debt payments, exchange-rate policy, low reserves, the 2022 depreciation, debt-service suspension, and global shocks. It also identifies domestic policy missteps, including tax cuts, monetary financing and the chemical-fertiliser ban. This report is an analytical source written as part of an IMF-supported programme; examine its evidence and institutional role.

Source card C — Sri Lanka's executive director at the IMF, March 2023

In a statement accompanying the programme report, Sri Lanka's executive director also identifies external shocks, including the Easter attacks, COVID-19 and the war in Ukraine. The statement gives especially strong attention to policy missteps: tax cuts, continued monetary financing, low interest rates, subsidised pricing of petroleum and electricity, the fertiliser ban, and defending the exchange rate using reserves. It represents the authorities' perspective within the IMF process; it is not a neutral account simply because it is official.

Source card D — World Bank, October 2022

The World Bank described longstanding structural weaknesses compounded by shocks and policy mistakes. It noted the combined effects of COVID-19 and high commodity prices associated with the war in Ukraine, alongside debt and falling reserves. This contemporaneous statement is useful for context and comparison, but its short format does not provide the detailed evidence needed to test every causal link.

Event cards and timeline worksheet

Event cards (place in chronological order; some processes overlap)

  1. Before 2019: Public debt, reliance on imported fuel, limited export diversification and fiscal constraints made the economy vulnerable. These are broad structural conditions; each needs evidence and definition.
  2. April 2019: Easter Sunday attacks sharply disrupted tourism. The exact size and duration of the effect require tourism data; the event reduced visitor activity.
  3. Late 2019: The government enacted large tax cuts. The IMF later estimated revenue losses exceeding 2% of GDP; the policy choice affected public revenue.
  4. 2020–21: COVID-19 sharply reduced tourism and economic activity. Fiscal deficits and external pressures grew; official data and later IMF reports describe the period.
  5. 2021: The government announced a ban on chemical fertiliser imports, later reversed. The policy aimed to move toward organic farming; the rapid change contributed to agricultural disruption, though the scale of its effect should be tested rather than assumed to explain the whole crisis.
  6. From April 2021: The exchange rate was effectively fixed/managed while foreign currency remained under pressure; reserves were used and foreign exchange became scarce.
  7. February 2022 onward: Russia's invasion of Ukraine was followed by higher international fuel and food prices. This increased import costs; it interacted with Sri Lanka's existing vulnerabilities.
  8. March–April 2022: The rupee depreciated sharply; shortages and queues worsened. On 12 April, authorities announced a temporary suspension of external debt service.
  9. 2022: Severe shortages and power cuts triggered protests and political upheaval. These were outcomes and feedback into economic activity, not the original cause of every vulnerability.

Causal-chain worksheet and perspective cards

Build a multi-causal explanation

Use these nodes, then add or revise them: reduced tourism receipts; lower foreign-currency income; tax cuts and lower government revenue; deficits and debt; external debt payments; use of reserves; low foreign-exchange availability; higher global fuel and food prices; import costs; exchange-rate depreciation; more expensive imports and foreign-currency obligations; shortages and power cuts; household hardship and protests.

Draw at least six arrows and label each with a mechanism (for example, “fewer visitor receipts reduce one source of foreign currency”). Include at least two domestic policy decisions and one external shock. Mark a link with “?” if evidence is incomplete. Add one possible feedback: shortages can reduce production and income, which can make public finances or foreign-currency inflows harder. State what evidence would test it.

Perspective card 1 — IMF staff analysis

Ask: What evidence does the staff report use? Which domestic policies and external shocks does it identify? What policy recommendations follow from its analysis? What is the IMF's institutional role in a lending programme?

Perspective card 2 — Sri Lankan executive director

Ask: Which causes receive greatest emphasis? Which facts overlap with the staff report? Does the statement offer evidence, interpretation, or both? What additional independent data could check its claims?

Claim test

  1. Write one shared claim both perspectives accept.
  2. Write one difference in emphasis or interpretation.
  3. Name one claim requiring data beyond these statements.
  4. Explain why an official source can be useful and still have a perspective.

Individual reconstruction worksheet

Write 150–180 words

Explain how Sri Lanka's 2022 crisis emerged from interacting causes. Include: one earlier vulnerability; two domestic decisions or policy choices; one external shock; at least two mechanisms linking them; two source cards by letter; one uncertainty or alternative interpretation. Avoid writing “X caused everything.” Distinguish what the sources report from your own explanation.

Sentence starters: “One vulnerability was …”; “This mattered because …”; “A later shock interacted with it when …”; “Source B supports …, while Source C places more emphasis on …”; “I am less certain about the link between … and … because …”

Language bridge

English pathway: Read the case reader in English and compare the two English IMF texts. Connect the case to Austria by writing one contrast question about why Austria and Sri Lanka may have different buffers against an import-price shock; use no stereotypes and name evidence needed to answer it. For an original-reading extension, read the IMF staff report's “Recent Developments” section, paragraphs 2–4, and the executive director statement's paragraph beginning “In the meantime, the government expressed…”. Extract one shared claim and one difference in emphasis, with paragraph reference. Glossary: reserves = Reserven; foreign exchange = Fremdwährung/Devisen; fiscal deficit = Budgetdefizit; depreciation = Abwertung; external debt service = Bedienung/Auszahlung von Auslandsschulden.

What to hand in

Individual reconstruction, 150–180 words, on paper or through the teacher's assignment platform. Success criteria: one vulnerability, two domestic decisions, one external shock, two explained mechanisms, two source-card references, and one uncertainty or alternative interpretation.

Exit ticket

Which connection in your explanation has the strongest evidence? Name the evidence.

Which single-cause explanation did the sources make you question, and why?

After class · up to 25 minutes

Spend up to 20 minutes. Pick one arrow from your causal map. Write what evidence would support it, what evidence could weaken it, and which source or dataset might provide that evidence.
