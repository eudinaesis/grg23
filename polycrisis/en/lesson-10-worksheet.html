Your 100-minute lesson

  1. 10 min · Read the region

    Read Case reader: Sonnfeld region. Mark two dependencies and one conflict; deliver a four-link map.

  2. 15 min · Compare ownership models

    Use Ownership cards and Stakeholder cards. Match each model to decision power, risk and likely benefit; record a comparison with one uncertainty.

  3. 15 min · Build a budget plan

    Use Sonnfeld budget table, Project menu and scoring. Choose projects up to 100 transition points and meet the minimums; fill the Council plan and score record.

  4. 20 min · Regional council simulation

    Follow Complete Regional Council rules and role cards. Complete the Council plan and score record with signed plan and one dissent note.

  5. 5 min · Break

    Take a five-minute break.

  6. 15 min · Stress-test the plan

    Use Shock card and score worksheet plus Council plan and score record. Recalculate and deliver before/after values with who bears the shock.

  7. 10 min · Compare with EU policy

    Read Source comparison: EU Just Transition. Find the stated purpose and one limitation; compare with the fictional budget.

  8. 10 min · Individual judgement

    Use Council plan and score record for your individual recommendation: explain ownership, distribution, shock result and one evidence gap.

Materials

Case reader: Sonnfeld region

Fictional case reader (teacher-written; about 300 words)

Sonnfeld is a fictional Austrian region of 85,000 people. It includes a small industrial town, farms, forest communities and a district-heating plant that burns imported gas. A factory that makes metal parts employs 900 people and uses a lot of electricity. Local households want reliable heat and manageable bills. The regional government wants lower emissions, stable tax income and jobs that young people can find locally.

The plant and factory are connected to the wider economy. If the plant closes before replacement heat is ready, households and businesses could face shortages or higher costs. If it stays open for longer, emissions continue and imported fuel remains a risk. A new wind and solar project could reduce exposure to fuel imports, but it needs grid upgrades, land agreements and time. A battery and heat-pump programme could help shift demand, while insulation lowers the amount of energy needed. These are mechanisms to investigate; the case gives no measured emissions or price forecast.

Ownership changes who decides and who carries financial risk. A private company may raise investment quickly and be accountable to investors. A municipal utility can coordinate infrastructure with local needs, but public budgets bear project risks. A cooperative can give residents a voice and share returns, but may find it harder to raise large sums. These are possible trade-offs, not guarantees. Contracts, regulation, access to capital and management matter too.

The council has 100 transition points to spend. The points are fictional planning units, not euros. The package must keep heat reliable, provide a pathway for affected workers, and include at least one emissions-reduction project. Groups will represent different residents and institutions. They may disagree over where projects are built, who owns them, how much is spent on retraining, and whether temporary support should go to workers, households, or the factory. A good plan explains who benefits and who bears risk; it also names what new evidence would be needed before real investment.

Ownership cards

Possible models: no model guarantees a good outcome

  • Private energy company: investors provide capital and expect a return. It may make decisions quickly. Risk: profits may leave the region and investors may reject projects with low expected returns. Ask what regulation and public-service contract applies.
  • Municipal utility: the municipality owns the provider. It can coordinate local heat, grid and planning. Risk: cost overruns can fall on public budgets; local political choices may favour some groups.
  • Energy cooperative: member residents jointly own a project and share voting rights. It can increase participation and local returns. Risk: capital and expertise may be limited; renters or lower-income households may be underrepresented.

For each, distinguish: who invests, who decides, who receives income, who bears losses, and who can be excluded.

Stakeholder cards

  • Factory workers: value secure jobs, fair notice and paid retraining; worry that new jobs may require different skills or be far away.
  • Low-income renters: want affordable reliable heating and help with building upgrades; they do not own the buildings and cannot choose major renovations alone.
  • Municipal council: wants dependable services, tax revenue and a plan that can be financed; it is accountable to residents.
  • Cooperative members: want local ownership and transparent accounts; they cannot promise that every resident can invest.
  • Grid operator: must maintain safe, reliable supply; new connections require capacity and planning. It cannot guarantee a construction date without studies.

Minimum conditions: reliability at least 1; worker security at least 2; climate at least 2; household protection at least 1. Points are additive. Project requirements must be met or that project's effect is zero. A package failing any minimum is not eligible for the council endorsement. All values are invented for play.

Complete Regional Council rules and role cards

Rules (20 minutes)

  1. Form five groups and assign roles. Each gets two minutes to read and chooses a spokesperson and note-taker.
  2. Round 1: three minutes to announce a priority. Round 2: seven minutes to negotiate a package costing at most 100 PU. Round 3: three minutes to make amendments; then vote.
  3. A package passes with at least four of five role votes, all four minimum conditions met, and every project prerequisite fulfilled. If none passes, record the closest plan and unmet conditions; do not invent points.
  4. Write project, cost, owner, prerequisites, votes and dissent. A dissenting role may attach one sentence.

Roles

  • Regional council: wants a deliverable plan and stable services. You control the 100 PU, but cannot raise more.
  • Workers' group: will support a plan with worker security at least 3 and a signed placement plan; oppose an unplanned early closure.
  • Renters' and household group: wants protection at least 2 and insists the insulation project has a renter-access plan; cannot invest in property it does not own.
  • Municipal utility: supports district-heat conversion if the 5 PU maintenance reserve is included; can co-own no more than two projects.
  • Cooperative and local businesses: support local projects and transparent ownership. Will support private investment only if contracts publish local benefit and consultation terms.

Debrief

Which ownership model had decision power? Which risks were shifted to workers, taxpayers, investors or residents? Did a faster project necessarily make the best transition? The point scores are teaching assumptions, not forecasts; actual projects require engineering, finance, environmental assessment and democratic consent.

Shock card and score worksheet

Draw one card after the agreement

  • Grid delay: if your plan includes wind/solar or cooperative solar, subtract 1 reliability this round. The project remains in the plan.
  • Factory order loss: if your plan has worker security below 3, subtract 1 worker security. If it has a signed placement plan, no subtraction.
  • Cold winter and gas price spike: if your plan lacks household protection, subtract 1 reliability and 1 household protection. Temporary bill support prevents the household-protection deduction, not the reliability deduction.

Teacher chooses one card or lets a student draw randomly. Apply only the listed change. Note who experiences the effect. Discuss that one card cannot represent real probability or the full set of simultaneous shocks.

Source comparison: EU Just Transition

The European Commission says its Just Transition Mechanism supports regions, workers and communities especially affected by the transition to a greener economy. Its Coal Regions in Transition initiative supports regional strategies and exchange. This is a real policy framework; Sonnfeld's 100 PU are invented and do not represent an EU allocation.

Language bridge

English pathway

Austria connection: compare Sonnfeld with an Austrian district-heating or industrial region using a local government, Statistik Austria or Austrian energy-agency source selected by the teacher. Do not infer local job effects from the fictional scores. Original-reading extension: read the European Commission's “EU coal regions in transition” page. Use the paragraph beginning “To ensure that no region...” and the following paragraph describing the Just Transition Mechanism; summarize one stated purpose and one limitation of the page's evidence.

What to hand in

Individual, 350–450 words: recommend a Sonnfeld package. Include cost arithmetic, ownership model, one worker or household consequence, the result after the Shock card, and one evidence gap. Criteria: all thresholds checked; distribution and risk explained; distinguish fictional score from real forecast.
