Your 100-minute lesson

    Read ‘Case reader: A label that must earn its place’ and sort the ‘Scenario cards’ into simultaneous, interaction, or insufficient evidence. Record one reason per card.

  2. 15 min · Learn the test

  3. 20 min · Map a case

    In groups, use ‘Scenario cards’ and ‘Polycrisis test, map key, and mechanism cards’ to draw a map with at least four nodes, two named mechanisms, one alternative explanation, and one uncertainty.

  4. 5 min · Break

    Take a five-minute break.

  5. 20 min · Stress-test the map

    Exchange maps. Apply the ‘Two interpretations and map audit’ material: challenge one arrow, identify one missing actor, and mark one claim that needs a source. Return a revised map.

  6. 15 min · Debate the label

    Read ‘Two interpretations and map audit’. Hold a structured discussion: one student argues that polycrisis adds value; another argues that the term risks hiding causes or responsibility. Cite a card and switch positions.

  7. 10 min · Individual judgement

    Write a short judgement using the ‘Verdict frame’ in ‘Two interpretations and map audit’: Does the scenario qualify as a polycrisis on the evidence supplied? What evidence could change your answer?

  8. 5 min · Exit

    Answer both exit questions individually.

Materials

Case reader: A label that must earn its place

A label that must earn its place

People often use crisis to mean a serious problem or a moment of danger. Polycrisis adds a claim: several crises interact. The word is useful only if we can show how. Adam Tooze has described a polycrisis as a situation in which distinct crises interact so that the whole is more dangerous or harder to manage than the parts considered separately. The definition directs attention to connections, not simply to the number of alarming headlines.

Imagine that a drought reduces a harvest. That is one shock. If lower harvests reduce export earnings for a country that depends on food exports, this may make it harder to pay for imported fuel. If fuel becomes scarce or expensive, transport and fertiliser costs may rise in the next growing season. The links could reinforce one another. But each link needs a mechanism and evidence. A government might have reserves, alternative crops, or access to credit; those buffers could weaken the chain. The example is a model for reasoning, not a statement that a particular country experienced this exact sequence.

A causal map helps make the argument visible. Boxes name conditions or outcomes; arrows state a mechanism such as “raises the cost of”, “reduces access to”, or “increases pressure on”. A feedback loop occurs when an effect returns to influence an earlier condition. More arrows do not mean more truth. A map should show alternatives, time, and uncertainty, and should distinguish observed facts from an author’s interpretation.

The idea also raises questions of power. Who caused a shock? Who can respond? Who has to absorb the loss? A country’s exposure may reflect decisions made elsewhere as well as its own policies. Two groups in one country can also have very different resources. A map that says only “the world is interconnected” explains little unless it identifies actors and choices.

There is a serious criticism: crisis language can make ordinary political choices seem like unavoidable emergencies. It can blur responsibility, exaggerate novelty, or place very different problems in one basket. So we will test the concept rather than adopt it as a slogan. Ask: what are the separate problems? What connects them? Is the interaction consequential? What evidence supports the arrows? Would another concept explain the case better? A strong answer may be “polycrisis”, “a linked set of risks”, or “several problems that should remain distinct”.

Polycrisis test, map key, and mechanism cards

  1. Name at least two distinct problems. Do not assume that events are crises just because they are serious.
  2. State the interaction: what changes because the problems meet?
  3. Show the mechanism, actors, place, and sequence.
  4. Ask whether the interaction materially changes the scale, duration, distribution, or manageability of harm.
  5. Check evidence, alternative explanations, buffers, and uncertainty.
  6. Ask what the label adds compared with a narrower description.

Two interpretations and map audit

Both are arguments to evaluate, not official answers.
Interpretation A: the concept helpsInterpretation B: the concept can mislead
Separate institutions and news sections can hide interactions. A map makes shared mechanisms visible and helps identify where intervention could reduce several risks.A very broad label can imply that everything is connected, conceal who made decisions, and make political choices sound unavoidable. A precise case study may explain more.

Map audit: Circle an arrow whose mechanism is unclear. Add one missing actor. Mark one claim that needs a source. Identify a buffer or alternative pathway. Ask whether the map explains something beyond “these things happened at the same time”.

Verdict frame: “I would / would not call this a polycrisis because… The strongest supported interaction is… The weakest link is… Evidence that could change my view is…”

Language bridge

English pathway: Use the English materials and make the Austrian connection by adding an Austria-relevant actor (municipality, energy supplier, federal government, workers, or consumers) to one map. Original-reading extension: In Tooze’s Chartbook #130, read the paragraph beginning “A polycrisis is…” (locate that exact opening on the page). In your own words, state the definition’s key test and one limitation of a definition alone.

Key terms: interaction = one problem changes the effects of another; mechanism = the process connecting cause and effect; buffer = something that reduces or delays an effect; exposure = likelihood or degree of being affected.

Exit ticket
