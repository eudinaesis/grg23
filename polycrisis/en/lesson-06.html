Teacher notes

Learning goals

In this fictional simulation, a global supply disruption forces three regions to compete for liquefied natural gas (LNG). The rules make price and need visible, but deliberately simplify real markets. Your job is to understand who can bid, who goes without, and what cooperation could change.

  • Explain how a shared supply shock can transmit across regions through trade and prices.
  • Compare ability to pay with social need and identify who bears consequences.
  • Use simulation evidence while identifying what the simplified model leaves out.

Your 100-minute lesson

  1. 12 min

    LNG in the chain

    Read the case reader What LNG can and cannot do and complete the first two boxes of the Supply chain worksheet.

  2. 13 min

    Understand the game

    Read the complete Simulation rules, Region cards, and Event card. Each team records its starting budget, need, and bid ceiling before play.

  3. 20 min

    Round 1: compete

    Run Round 1 using the Auction sheet. Record units awarded, prices, unmet needs, and remaining budget for every region.

  4. 5 min

    Break

    Take a five-minute break.

  5. 20 min

    Round 2: cooperate

    Apply the new-round supply and budget rules. Negotiate one voluntary cooperation agreement, then allocate units using the same auction procedure.

  6. 20 min

    Debrief and map

    Use the Results table, Causal map worksheet, and Polycrisis newsletter argument card. Compare rounds and the authors’ claim; draw at least four labelled arrows, including one cross-region connection, and write one question that would test the claim.

  7. 10 min

    Individual explanation

    Write a 120–160-word explanation: who went without, why, what cooperation changed, and one limitation of this model. Submit to the teacher's assignment platform or on paper.

Materials

Case reader: What LNG can and cannot do

Case reader: What LNG can and cannot do

Natural gas can be transported through pipelines or shipped as liquefied natural gas. LNG is cooled until it becomes liquid, loaded onto specialised ships, then warmed back into gas at a terminal. This can connect buyers and sellers that a pipeline does not join. It does not make gas instantly available everywhere: liquefaction plants, ships, terminals, pipelines, contracts and time all matter.

When buyers seek more gas than sellers can provide quickly, competition can raise prices. A buyer with more money may offer a higher price, reserve cargoes through contracts, or pay for infrastructure. A poorer buyer may be unable to match that offer even if households or hospitals need fuel. This is one way that purchasing power and human need can diverge. It is also possible for high prices to encourage conservation, production or investment, but those responses take time and can impose serious costs.

After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, European countries sought alternatives to Russian pipeline gas, including LNG. The European Commission described joint purchasing, demand reduction and solidarity between member states as parts of its response. These measures operate within a world market: a purchase can improve one region's security while affecting the opportunities or prices faced by other buyers. That does not mean every European purchase directly caused a shortage elsewhere; the effects depend on supply, timing, infrastructure, contracts, and other buyers' choices.

This class game isolates one mechanism: unequal purchasing power under a limited supply. Its numbers, regions and bids are fictional. Real gas markets include long-term contracts, traders, storage, pipeline networks, technical constraints, different gas qualities, state-owned firms, and many suppliers and consumers. Real governments also face questions of fairness, emissions and energy alternatives. Therefore, the game can help us ask questions, but it cannot predict actual prices or establish what happened in a particular country.

As you play, watch both price and allocation. Ask whether a high bid represents greater need, greater ability to pay, or both. Then consider whether a rule for sharing supply could protect essential services without pretending that cooperation has no cost.

Simulation rules: fictional LNG market

All game data are fictional teaching data

Goal: Observe how bidding allocates scarce energy and how one cooperation agreement changes outcomes. There is no individual winner; the class compares collective and unequal results.

Teams and starting cards: Make three teams. If the class is larger, split each region into subteams that share one region card and make one joint bid. Region A (High-income importers): need 5 units; budget 30 tokens; value essential services 4 points per unit. Region B (Middle-income importers): need 5; budget 18 tokens; 4 points per unit. Region C (Low-income importers): need 5; budget 9 tokens; 4 points per unit. One energy unit is a fictional block of supply; tokens are fictional budget points.

Supply and prices: Round 1 has 9 units available. The auctioneer sells units one at a time, beginning with a minimum bid of 1 token per unit. Each region secretly writes its maximum affordable bid per unit and its desired number of units, then reveals both. Each unit goes to the highest remaining bid; ties are settled by a drawn team order. Winning teams pay their bid for each unit. No team may exceed its budget. Bidding stops when all 9 units are sold or all teams pass. Unsatisfied need equals 5 minus units received.

Round 2: A fictional shipping interruption reduces supply to 8 units. Each region's remaining budget is its start budget minus Round 1 spending, plus 6 tokens for a new budget period. Teams may negotiate one agreement for three minutes: (a) reserve 2 units for essential services across regions, allocating one each to two named regions; (b) transfer up to 3 tokens from one region to another; or (c) jointly set a maximum price of 2 tokens per unit. The agreement must be written and accepted by all teams. Then each team receives its own Round 2 need card: 4 units each. Follow the same bidding process for remaining supply, with reserved units allocated first and counted against each recipient's Round 2 need. Teams pay auction price for auctioned units; reserved units cost 1 token each.

Scoring: Each unit received earns 4 service points, up to the round's need. Each unmet unit costs 3 hardship points. Subtract tokens spent from the region's service points. Net model score = service points − hardship points − tokens spent. Record the three components as well as the net; scores are a model, not a moral measure of lives.

Procedure: 1) Assign auctioneer/timekeeper and region teams. 2) Play Round 1 in 8 minutes. 3) Record every allocation and payment. 4) Apply interruption, budget reset and need cards. 5) Negotiate for 3 minutes; the agreement expires after Round 2. 6) Play and record Round 2 in 8 minutes. 7) Debrief with the questions below. No borrowing, side payments, or hidden deals. If a budget is insufficient, that bid is invalid. If a team passes, the next highest valid bid wins.

Auction clarification

Submit one fixed whole-token price per unit for each round, and a desired quantity no larger than your unmet need. This is the actual price paid for every unit won, not an instruction to the auctioneer to bid on your behalf. Keep the price fixed during that round. Award units in descending bid order until each bidder reaches its desired quantity or can no longer afford its next unit; then move to the next valid bidder. Use the drawn tie order throughout the round. Record scores separately for each round: service points and hardship reset at the start of Round 2; only money carries over under the stated budget rule. Compare the two round scores and allocations separately; do not add them into a course grade or a moral ranking.

Region cards, auction sheet and debrief

Cut or assign region cards

  • Region A — high-income importers: Round 1 need 5 units; budget 30 tokens; Round 2 need 4; new-period addition +6. You may bid up to your current budget, divided by desired units. Ask whether a strong budget should determine access to all supply.
  • Region B — middle-income importers: Round 1 need 5; budget 18; Round 2 need 4; new-period addition +6. Your need is as large as A's, but your budget is smaller. Ask what alternative rules could recognise need.
  • Region C — low-income importers: Round 1 need 5; budget 9; Round 2 need 4; new-period addition +6. Your budget is limited. Explain how unmet energy need could affect households and services, without treating the card as a real country's data.

Debrief questions

  1. Did the auction allocate by need, ability to pay, or a mixture? Point to the result table.
  2. Who paid most? Who had unmet need? What could not be inferred from those facts?
  3. What did the agreement change, and who paid its cost?
  4. Which real features were absent: contracts, storage, infrastructure, time, emissions, or alternatives?

Causal map

Draw at least four arrows. Label each with a mechanism, such as “higher bid wins cargo” or “terminal capacity limits delivery.” Include one arrow crossing regions and mark any uncertain connection with a question mark.

Polycrisis newsletter argument card: gas bidding and unequal purchasing power

Authors’ argument (teacher-written paraphrase; not a quotation)

In their October 2022 introduction, Kate Mackenzie and Tim Sahay argue that Europe and richer East Asian economies competed for limited gas supplies while poorer countries faced fuel shortages and difficulty affording US-dollar-priced imports. Their point is that energy security in one region can connect to financial and human hardship elsewhere. This is an argument about linked mechanisms, not proof that any one buyer caused a particular shortage.

Compare with the official evidence: The European Commission page describes proposed European measures, including joint purchasing, solidarity and demand reduction. It documents a policy agenda; it does not establish the newsletter authors’ distributional claim by itself.

  1. What link do Mackenzie and Sahay propose between wealthy buyers and hardship elsewhere?
  2. What additional data would show whether a particular buyer changed prices or availability for another country?
  3. Does the simulation model need, ability to pay, or both? What does it leave out?

Language bridge

English pathway: Use the case reader and game rules in English. Make the Austrian connection by finding one official Austrian source about gas supply, storage or energy consumption; note the source date and explain why an Austrian buyer is connected to a regional market. For an original-reading extension, read the European Commission page “Commission makes additional proposals to fight high energy prices and ensure security of supply” (18 October 2022), focusing on the paragraph beginning “The Commission is proposing today a new emergency regulation…” and the following list of measures. Identify one cooperation measure and one demand measure. Glossary: liquefied natural gas (LNG) = Flüssigerdgas; purchasing power = Kaufkraft; supply = Angebot/Versorgung; unmet need = ungedeckter Bedarf; allocation = Zuteilung.

What to hand in

Individual explanation, 120–160 words, paper or teacher's assignment platform. It must cite one recorded result from each round, explain ability to pay versus need, state what cooperation changed, and name one important limit of the simulation.

Exit ticket

  • What determined access to supply in Round 1? Use one result as evidence.
  • What one rule would you change to make the outcome fairer, and what trade-off might that create?

After class · up to 25 minutes

Spend up to 20 minutes. Re-read the simulation results and write two alternative rules for distributing scarce supply. For each, state who benefits and one possible cost or unintended effect.
