Batteries: who captures the value?

Can a country gain more from a battery supply chain than the minerals it exports?

Teacher notes

Learning goals

A battery is assembled through a chain of mining, refining, material production, cell manufacturing, pack assembly and recycling. Countries may want jobs and know-how at several stages, while companies and consumers need reliable products. We will map a chain and negotiate a fictional investment offer.

  • Describe several stages of a battery value chain and how disruptions travel through it.
  • Distinguish mineral extraction from refining and manufacturing value capture.
  • Assess an investment deal using jobs, environmental limits, technology learning and supply risk.

Materials

Case reader: Kalo and the battery chain

Fictional case reader (teacher-written; about 300 words)

Kalo is a fictional country that exports lithium ore and has a young workforce, a deep-water port and unreliable electricity. A foreign consortium proposes a battery-materials and cell-assembly complex near the port. The company says it could create jobs and make Kalo a regional supplier. The government wants higher incomes, reliable power, skills and local firms that can sell to the plant. Residents near the proposed site want clean water, consultation and a way to share in benefits.

A battery supply chain has different stages. Minerals are mined; ores may be concentrated and refined; active materials are produced; cells are manufactured; cells are assembled into packs and installed in vehicles or storage; batteries are later repaired, reused or recycled. Each stage needs different skills, infrastructure, capital, energy and environmental safeguards. A country that exports raw material may receive export earnings but have little control over later stages. Building a factory may create capabilities, but it can also depend on imported equipment, technology, finance and customer orders.

The International Energy Agency's 2025 critical-minerals outlook describes concentration across parts of mineral processing and battery supply chains, and notes that new battery chemistries can shift which minerals are important. Its analysis is global; it does not predict Kalo's project outcomes. In this exercise, students use an official source to distinguish what is reported from what is only proposed in the fictional offer.

Joining a global value chain does not automatically produce broad development. A plant may import most inputs, repatriate profits or create jobs with limited local learning. On the other hand, strict local-content requirements may raise costs, delay construction or deter investment. Policy choices can include training, local supplier development, environmental standards, public reporting, tax terms and technology partnerships. The class will negotiate a deal under a fixed fictional envelope. No side can make a real investment promise. The aim is to decide what evidence and conditions would make the offer worth accepting, and to notice who has bargaining power when a country needs capital and firms need resources.

Battery Chain cards and worksheet

Cut out and arrange; cards are teacher-written summaries

  1. Mining: extract ore or brine; questions include land, water and worker safety.
  2. Concentration/refining: turn mined material into battery-grade chemicals; needs processing plants, energy, water and technical controls.
  3. Active materials: make cathode/anode materials; chemistry choice changes which inputs matter.
  4. Cell manufacturing: produce cells; requires specialised equipment, quality control, capital and reliable power.
  5. Pack and product assembly: combine cells with electronics and safety systems; connect to vehicles or grid storage.
  6. Use, repair and recycling: maintain products, recover materials and manage waste; collection and safe processing matter.

Worksheet: draw arrows between stages. Label each arrow with one possible flow (material, money, knowledge, energy, waste or risk). Add a disruption point and identify one downstream effect. Not every battery uses the same chemistry or exact inputs.

IEA evidence cards and Claim audit table

Paraphrased official-source evidence; Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025

  • Evidence 1: the IEA reports that critical-mineral supply chains can remain vulnerable even when overall projected supply appears adequate, because production and processing are concentrated and shocks can disrupt flows.
  • Evidence 2: the IEA describes emerging battery chemistries as changing which mineral supply chains matter; alternatives to nickel-based chemistries have different input profiles and risks.
  • Evidence 3: the IEA notes that battery supply chains include more than mining: downstream stages such as cells and materials can be concentrated in particular places.

Complete Kalo investment negotiation rules and role cards

Rules (20 minutes)

  1. Four groups take roles. Read for two minutes; each names its non-negotiable and one tradable condition.
  2. Negotiate for 10 minutes. Each side may propose up to three conditions. The consortium accepts or rejects each and may accept no more than three. Government approval requires water monitoring and at least two accepted conditions. Workers support a deal with the training fund or technology partnership. Residents support it with monitoring plus local suppliers or community equity. These preferences may lead to signed dissent if unmet.
  3. After signing, apply each accepted term’s listed benefit and company cost/control penalty. The consortium signs only if accepted terms require no more than 23 total company tokens and no more than 1 control penalty. Count token contributions listed in the menu; the tax floor counts as 8 tokens of foregone revenue. Other roles may still dissent. Report separate scores; do not add them into one total.
  4. Use four minutes to write a term sheet. If no agreement, record the best offer and unresolved issue. Do not invent terms or score effects.

Term menu (propose up to three)

  • Training fund: company pays 10 fictional tokens; workers +2 skills.
  • Local supplier programme: company spends 8 tokens on supplier qualification; local firms +2 capability.
  • Water monitoring and public data: company pays 5 tokens; residents +2 trust; project gets environmental consent.
  • Profit-tax floor: company accepts a fictional 15% minimum effective tax (counts as 8 tokens of foregone revenue); public budget +2.
  • Technology partnership: company and local technical college share a pilot line; company pays 3 tokens; workers +1 skills and firms +1 capability; consortium incurs 1 control penalty.
  • Power reliability contract: government reserves capacity and company pays 6 tokens toward backup; reliability +2; public budget −1 token.
  • Community equity share: consortium reserves 5% fictional project shares for a community trust; residents +2 benefit; consortium incurs 1 control penalty.

Role cards

  • Kalo government: want jobs, taxes and reliable power; cannot spend more than 1 token. Require water monitoring and at least two accepted terms. You may reject the offer.
  • Consortium: want a predictable investment and control of core technology. Accept no more than three terms; reject technology partnership or tax floor if you choose, but then the government may reject your deal.
  • Workers and technical college: prefer the training fund or technology partnership; support either one. You cannot promise the factory will hire every trainee.
  • Residents and local suppliers: require water monitoring; prefer community equity or supplier programme. You may accept one of those as a compromise, but state what remains unresolved.

Deal score: report separately: workers' skills (0–3), local firm capability (0–3), resident trust/benefit (0–4), public budget (+/− tokens), reliability (0–2), company token cost (0–23 limit), control penalties (0–1 limit). Do not combine into a single “best” score.

Debrief

Who had the ability to walk away? Which conditions change capabilities rather than only immediate revenue? Could a government enforce the terms? What data would be needed on water, wages, tax and ownership? This game simplifies bargaining, assumes fixed company costs, leaves global market prices out, and gives residents only one role.

Disruption cards and application rules

After signing, draw one card; apply the stated effect

  • Processing-equipment delay: if the agreement lacks a technology partnership, project start is delayed; subtract 1 reliability. With partnership, no score change, but note that delay risk is reduced rather than eliminated.
  • Water-monitoring alert: if no monitoring term was accepted, residents' trust −2 and government must pause approval. If monitoring was accepted, publish the result; the card does not say whether water is safe.
  • Battery chemistry shift: if the deal relies only on lithium ore exports and has no supplier programme or technology partnership, local firm capability −1. Explain why a change in chemistry could alter demand; do not claim lithium becomes useless.

Only apply the relevant card effect. Discuss that these are possible stress tests, not probabilities or predictions.

Exit ticket

  • Which battery stage might create capabilities beyond mineral exports?
  • Name one reason the supply-chain map does not by itself prove a good investment.

After class · up to 25 minutes

In 20 minutes, add a 120-word note to your term sheet: what should happen if the plant misses its training or water-monitoring promise? Use the role cards and state what evidence verifies compliance.
