Your 100-minute lesson

  1. 10 min · Three households

    Read the Household cards in the material Three households and mark who faces the greatest risk from a sudden bill increase. Write one reason for your choice.

  2. 12 min · How policy tools work

    Read the case reader Energy prices and public choices and the Policy tool cards. In the comparison table, note who receives help, who pays, and one limitation for each tool.

  3. 18 min · Evidence check

    Use the Evidence cards and the Recommendation worksheet. In groups, select two pieces of evidence and complete the worksheet's claim, mechanism, beneficiary, cost, and uncertainty boxes.

  4. 5 min · Break

    Take a five-minute break.

  5. 25 min · Policy hearing

    Use the Role cards and Hearing rules. Each group presents a two-minute recommendation, answers one challenge, and records one change it would make after hearing others.

  6. 20 min · Individual portfolio entry 1

    Complete the Individual policy memo worksheet using the evidence and hearing. Submit on the teacher's assignment platform or on paper; the website does not collect work.

  7. 10 min · Exit and reflection

    Answer both Exit questions individually. Add one sentence to your portfolio memo explaining what evidence might change your recommendation.

Materials

Case reader: Energy prices and public choices

Case reader: Energy prices and public choices

Energy is used directly for heating, cooking and transport, and indirectly to make and move many goods. If the price of gas or electricity rises, a household may face a larger bill. A factory, shop or farm may also pay more to operate. Some businesses pass part of the extra cost to customers; others reduce production, delay investment or accept lower profits. The effect travels through linked systems.

In 2021–22, Europe faced unusually high wholesale gas and electricity prices. The European Commission described the emergency measures adopted in 2022 as responses to high prices and supply concerns. Its later review says measures included lower electricity demand, temporary limits on some producers' market revenues, and rules allowing public intervention in retail prices. The Commission judged that the interventions contributed to calmer markets alongside other changes. This is an institutional assessment, not proof that one measure alone caused the improvement.

Governments must make choices under uncertainty. A targeted cash payment can help households with low incomes while leaving the market price visible. It requires a way to identify eligible people and may arrive too late. A broad price cap can limit bills quickly, but it can also subsidise high consumption and transfer costs to taxpayers or energy suppliers. Efficiency support—such as insulation or efficient heating—can reduce future energy use, but renovations take time and people who rent may not control the building.

These tools distribute costs differently. A policy can protect people from immediate hardship and still preserve an incentive to save energy. It can also create new problems: public borrowing, unfair access, weaker conservation signals, or delays in investment. A fair comparison asks who is protected, who pays, how quickly help arrives, what happens to energy use, and what the policy cannot solve. There is no automatic answer. Evidence about incomes, housing, energy use and the public budget matters, as do values about fairness and responsibility.

For this class, the fictional country and household cases are teaching models. The historical description of EU action is based on a European Commission review published in June 2023.

Policy tool cards and comparison table

Policy tool cards

  • Targeted cash support: The government pays eligible households a fixed amount. Possible strength: directs money to people with less ability to absorb a shock. Possible limit: eligibility rules miss some people; money does not directly lower the energy price.
  • Retail price cap: The government limits the price charged up to a defined level or quantity. Possible strength: quick, visible bill protection. Possible limit: if the state reimburses suppliers, public cost can rise; a lower price can weaken the incentive to conserve.
  • Efficiency investment: Grants or public works reduce heat loss or improve equipment. Possible strength: can lower future energy needs. Possible limit: slower effect; renters may depend on landlords; supply of workers and materials may constrain delivery.

Evidence cards

Evidence cards: read the date and the limits

  • EU emergency review: The European Commission's 5 June 2023 review says EU countries used electricity-demand reduction, a cap on certain inframarginal revenues, and retail price-setting interventions. The Commission assessed these measures as contributing to calmer energy markets alongside other measures adopted in 2022. Use this to establish what measures existed and how the Commission assessed them; do not claim it proves which single measure caused lower prices.
  • Austrian household energy: Statistics Austria publishes household energy-use data and a 2021/22 report on dimensions of energy poverty. The data describe patterns and measured hardship; they do not tell us automatically which policy is best. For this exercise, no numerical Austrian statistic is supplied, so do not invent one.
  • Reasoned inference: If a household has low income and cannot change its heating system, an immediate payment may be more usable than a renovation grant. This is a plausible inference from the fictional card, not a measured finding about all households.

Use at least one historical evidence card and one household-card detail. Label each statement as a fact, inference, or value judgement.

Role cards and hearing rules

Role cards (fictional perspectives)

  • Low-income renter representative: Prioritise quick help that reaches tenants. Ask how people are identified and whether heating remains affordable.
  • Small business association: Prioritise predictable bills and keeping local employers open. Ask who pays if suppliers are reimbursed.
  • Climate and energy-efficiency adviser: Prioritise lasting reductions in energy demand. Ask how immediate hardship is covered while work is planned.
  • Finance ministry analyst: Prioritise a policy the public budget can sustain. Ask what happens if high prices last longer than expected.
  • Energy supplier: Prioritise reliable supply and clear rules. Ask whether the policy covers costs and supports conservation.

Hearing rules

  1. Groups choose or receive one role. Roles are fictional and do not represent every person in a group.
  2. Prepare a recommendation using one or more tools. You may combine them, but say how the combination is funded.
  3. Give a two-minute statement: name the problem, the tool, the intended beneficiaries, the payer, and one limitation.
  4. Another group asks one evidence-based challenge. Reply by clarifying, qualifying, or revising your proposal.
  5. Do not disclose anyone's actual household finances. Critique policies, not classmates.

Recommendation and individual memo worksheets

Group recommendation worksheet

  1. Our claim: What should the fictional government do in the next year?
  2. Mechanism: How does the measure change bills, income, energy use, or investment?
  3. Beneficiaries: Who gains first? Who might be missed?
  4. Cost and payer: Who funds or bears the cost?
  5. Evidence: What two pieces support our reasoning?
  6. Uncertainty and trade-off: What might not work as planned?
  7. Revision: What did we change after the hearing?

Individual policy memo (180–220 words)

Write a memo to the fictional energy minister. Recommend one tool or a clearly funded combination. Explain the mechanism, cite two supplied evidence items by title, identify who benefits and who bears a cost, and state one uncertainty. End with a sentence describing evidence that could change your view. You may argue for a different option from your group.

Language bridge

English pathway: Use the English reader and cards as the main text. Connect the case to Austria by opening Statistics Austria's household energy page and the 2021/22 energy-poverty report. Find one chart or definition that could help a minister decide whom to support; record its title, unit, date and one limitation. For an original-reading extension, read the European Commission page titled “Commission report: Emergency energy measures facilitated market improvement” (5 June 2023), focusing only on the paragraph beginning “In a report on the review of emergency interventions…” and the following examples. In two sentences, distinguish the Commission's account of measures from its judgement about their effects. Glossary: price cap = Preisobergrenze; targeted = gezielt; energy poverty = Energiearmut; efficiency = Effizienz; trade-off = Zielkonflikt.

What to hand in

Individual portfolio memo, 180–220 words, written on paper or submitted through the teacher's assignment platform. Success criteria: a clear recommendation; a correct mechanism; two supplied evidence items; named beneficiaries and cost bearers; one limitation or uncertainty; a sentence saying what evidence could change the recommendation.

Exit ticket

Which policy tool did you recommend, and through what mechanism should it help?

Who might still be left out, and what evidence would help you notice that?

After class · up to 25 minutes

Spend no more than 20 minutes. Re-read the Evidence cards and revise your individual memo by adding one sentence that distinguishes a fact, an inference, and a value judgement. Bring the revised memo to the next class.
