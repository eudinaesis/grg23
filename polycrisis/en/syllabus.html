Course question: How do crises in climate, energy, economics and politics interact, who has power to respond, and when does the word polycrisis help us understand events?

This 16-session elective is designed for students aged 15–18 in Austria. Each session is 100 minutes. Students work from concrete cases towards increasingly independent analysis. The course uses Adam Tooze’s crisis-mapping approach and questions raised by Phenomenal World writers as starting points for inquiry, not as authorities students must agree with.

Why these three cases?

  • Europe’s energy and cost-of-living shock: connects energy supply, prices, household effects, public policy and unequal international exposure. Students distinguish a chain of mechanisms from a vague list of simultaneous problems.
  • Sri Lanka’s 2022 crisis: a bounded historical case for investigating foreign exchange, imports, debt, tourism, domestic decisions and limited policy room. Students ask how external pressures and choices within the country interacted, without reducing the event to one cause.
  • The energy transition and battery supply chains: brings climate goals into conversation with industry, trade, finance, jobs and geopolitical competition. Students examine who may gain, who may bear costs, and what a policy response can change.

The cases recur throughout the course so students can apply the same tools and compare what the framework reveals. The 2022 material is historical: students should not treat it as a current account of conditions in Europe or Sri Lanka. The final sessions examine whether “polycrisis” explains more than more specific descriptions of interacting crises.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, students can:

  • explain a bounded case through a clearly described causal mechanism and distinguish a causal chain, a feedback loop, and mere coincidence;
  • construct and revise a crisis map, marking evidence, uncertainty, alternative explanations and connections that may reduce pressure;
  • compare how a crisis and proposed responses affect different households, groups, firms and countries;
  • identify relevant decision-makers, institutions, resources and constraints without treating a country as a single actor;
  • evaluate policy options against stated criteria, including trade-offs, implementation and possible unintended effects;
  • assess claims using evidence from at least four sources, including independent evidence and a contrasting argument;
  • make and defend a reasoned judgement about whether the polycrisis concept clarifies the chosen case.

Sixteen-session syllabus

The session sequence is designed for two 50-minute periods with a short pause. The course site links each week to a class page with its assignment and materials.

  1. Connected lives: trace links behind an everyday object, food item or energy service; make an initial map and name what is not yet known.
  2. What counts as a polycrisis? distinguish multiple problems from interacting crises; practise arrows with stated mechanisms and evidence.
  3. Europe’s energy shock: build a dated historical timeline and identify dependencies, disruptions and competing explanations.
  4. Prices and households: distinguish a change in the price level from a change in inflation; interpret a supplied chart and compare fictional household budgets.
  5. Protecting people: compare targeted support, price limits and efficiency investment in a policy hearing. Portfolio checkpoint 1: individual causal explanation with evidence and uncertainty.
  6. Unequal exposure: use a clearly labelled classroom simulation to explore competition for energy cargoes; debrief the assumptions and limits of the model.
  7. Sri Lanka, part I: assemble a case dossier to investigate tourism receipts, foreign exchange, imports and domestic decisions.
  8. Sri Lanka, part II: examine debt, currency and policy room through a fictional numerical exercise; distinguish the exercise’s invented figures from historical evidence. Portfolio checkpoint 2: source comparison and revised map.
  9. Climate damage and recovery: compare grants, loans and repayment pauses in a fictional recovery negotiation; consider distribution and financing constraints.
  10. The energy transition: design options for a fictional industrial region, balancing emissions goals, jobs, infrastructure and cost.
  11. Batteries and development: map a supply chain and compare the interests of workers, firms and governments in different positions.
  12. Cooperation and competition: negotiate investment, technology access and energy security; identify who can set or change the rules.
  13. Historical comparison and concept check: compare selected energy-crisis mechanisms across periods and ask what “polycrisis” adds or obscures. Portfolio checkpoint 3: a provisional concept judgement supported by evidence.
  14. Project workshop: narrow the question, select sources, create a first crisis map and receive formative feedback.
  15. Challenge and revise: peer-review evidence and reasoning, test an alternative explanation and complete the project draft.
  16. Chartbook and oral defence: present the investigation, answer questions and reflect on what the framework helped explain.

Assessment structure

The portfolio checkpoints in sessions 5, 8 and 13 are short individual pieces. They give feedback on explanation, evidence and judgement before the final project. The final investigation may be written individually or in pairs; each student must show individual understanding in the oral defence and reflection. A suggested final project is 1,000–1,200 words, with a crisis map, 2–3 explained charts, at least four sources, an independent evidence source and a contrasting argument. Students may conclude that the polycrisis label is only partly useful.

Assessment criteria and the four-level rubric are provided in the Project and assessment section. The rubric supports feedback; it does not prescribe a school grade conversion.
