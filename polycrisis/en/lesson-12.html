Teacher notes

Learning goals

Countries facing debt, energy and technology pressures choose partners. Cooperation can improve access to finance, markets or technology; dependence can reduce room to change policy. In this fictional case, the public grid programme costs 80 units. A separate private battery-plant offer cannot pay that public bill. Negotiate with this distinction clear.

  • Explain how trade, finance and technology ties can create both opportunity and dependence.
  • Distinguish cooperation from alignment and identify what a country can negotiate.
  • Evaluate a fictional partnership by gains, obligations, alternatives and who has voice.

Your 100-minute lesson

  1. 10 min

    Map the choices

  2. 15 min

  3. 15 min

    Use Asteria mandate and funding worksheet. Rank priorities and calculate public grid contributions and obligations; deliver the mandate.

  4. 20 min

    Three-party negotiation

    Follow Complete three-party negotiation rules and role cards. Record public grid funding separately from the South plant investment on Asteria signed agreement and risk map; deliver signed text and dissent.

  5. 5 min

    Break

    Take a five-minute break.

  6. 15 min

    Test the agreement

    Use Disruption cards and option-value table on Asteria signed agreement and risk map. Decide whether to review, exit or continue; deliver the revised risk map.

  7. 10 min

    Compare with historical non-alignment

    Read Source comparison: historical non-alignment and policy space and Phenomenal World argument card: non-alignment today. Distinguish historical principles from Sahay’s interpretation; record two similarities/differences and one question.

  8. 10 min

    Individual judgement

    Use Asteria signed agreement and risk map for your individual judgement: benefit, dependency, alternative and uncertainty.

Materials

Case reader: Asteria's partnership decision

Fictional case reader (teacher-written; about 300 words)

Asteria is a fictional middle-income country with a large solar-resource potential, expensive imported fuel and a government seeking more manufacturing jobs. It needs to upgrade its electricity grid and expand storage. Three partners offer different packages for two separate projects. The public grid programme costs 80 units. Partner North offers a 60-unit loan for grid technology, requiring repayment and a long-term service contract. The Regional Development Bank offers a 25-unit grant for the grid, but cannot fund the whole programme. Partner South offers 40 private units for a separate battery factory and training, in return for mineral supply and operational control. That private factory investment cannot be spent on the public grid. No offer is costless or complete.

A country can cooperate with several partners without joining a military or political bloc. But ties can still create dependence. A loan creates repayment obligations. A service contract can lock in one supplier. A guaranteed supply agreement may reduce flexibility if prices or technology change. On the other hand, refusing every agreement may delay infrastructure and leave fuel imports expensive. Bargaining power depends on alternatives, the value each side brings, financing needs, domestic capacity and the ability to enforce terms.

Historical non-alignment emerged during the Cold War, when states sought room for independent foreign policy amid rivalry between major powers. It was not simply a rule to avoid all relationships, nor did member governments always agree. The movement also called for cooperation and changes to unequal economic relations. Today, describing a country as “non-aligned” may refer to different policies and situations; a label alone does not tell us what a government actually does.

The classroom negotiation gives three fictional actors different priorities and incomplete information. Students must decide which conditions are essential, what can be traded, and how the agreement responds to a shock. They should ask who can walk away, whose interests are not represented, and whether a written safeguard can be enforced. The goal is not to choose a geopolitical camp. It is to explain how cooperation can expand options and how it can narrow them, while distinguishing documented facts from strategic claims made by an actor.

Position cards

  • Partner North: offers an LC 60-equivalent low-interest loan for grid technology. Wants a 12-year service contract for its supplier and a procurement preference. Says its equipment is reliable; ask for performance data and exit terms.
  • Partner South: offers a joint-venture battery-material plant, worker training and a 10-year purchase agreement for a share of Asteria's minerals. Wants stable supply and majority control of operations. Says the project builds local capability; ask what technology is transferred and who owns it.
  • Regional Development Bank: offers grant support worth 25 units and technical help for grid planning; no supply or exclusive-service clause. It cannot fund the entire expansion. Requires public procurement and environmental review.
  • Asteria civic coalition: wants reliable electricity, local jobs, public accountability and the ability to change suppliers. It has no power to sign treaties but can publish a dissent statement and demand consultation.

Complete three-party negotiation rules and role cards

Rules (20 minutes)

  1. Four groups take roles: Asteria government, Partner North, Partner South, Regional Development Bank. The teacher, or a pair of students assigned by the teacher, serves as civic observer and may issue one public challenge after round one; it is not an extra negotiating group.
  2. Each role reads for two minutes. Round 1 (5 minutes): bilateral offers. Round 2 (7 minutes): multilateral negotiation. Round 3 (4 minutes): draft and sign. Use only terms in the table and menu.
  3. A valid public-grid deal must provide 80 grid units and receive Asteria and the signature of every partner whose grid funding is counted. Count only North’s grid loan, the Bank’s grid grant and Asteria’s public budget. South’s 40 private units fund a separate battery plant and never count toward 80. Asteria may use up to 25 public units. The Bank requires open procurement, so it cannot combine with North’s procurement preference unless both renegotiate that condition. South may negotiate a separate plant memorandum, but it cannot close the grid funding gap. If no valid grid plan is agreed, record the gap; that is a valid outcome.
  4. Partners may refuse. If no deal, record the gap and each side's best alternative. Dissent may be attached. Do not invent funding or score a fictional deal as real-world fact.

Role cards

  • Asteria government: prioritize reliable energy and maintain choice of suppliers. You have 25 public units. You may accept one long-term/exclusive condition, not both; require a review clause after a major technology or price change.
  • Partner North: need a service contract and procurement preference to support the 60-unit loan. You may shorten service contract to 8 years if Asteria pays 5 additional public units for training. You cannot waive loan repayment.
  • Partner South: need 40% mineral supply for 10 years and majority operational control. You may reduce term to 6 years if Asteria grants joint governance and a published training target. The investment is private, not public grant money.
  • Regional Development Bank: offer 25 grant units if procurement is open, environmental review is public and Asteria contributes at least 10 units. You can combine with another offer but cannot guarantee it fills the full gap.
  • Civic observer: listen and note whose costs are invisible. After round 1, ask one question that a deal must answer. At the end, publish one dissent statement; you do not vote.

Debrief

Which actor had credible alternatives? Did combining partners reduce or increase dependence? What happens if a contract is legally binding but hard to enforce? The exercise freezes prices, assumes offers are available, excludes many domestic groups and simplifies legal institutions. Non-alignment is a historical and political concept, not a guarantee of neutrality or autonomy.

Disruption cards and option-value table

Draw one disruption after signing

  • Technology shift: storage technology changes. If a contract requires one supplier for 12 years, mark flexibility “low”; with review clause, “medium”; with no exclusive contract, “higher”. No numeric score: explain why.
  • Mineral price spike: if Asteria committed 40% output for 10 years, government receives the agreed supply benefit but loses option to sell that share elsewhere during the spike. Record both effects.
  • Public budget squeeze: if Asteria used more than 15 public units, reduce the contingency reserve by the excess over 15. Name a public service that might face pressure; do not assume a specific cut occurs.

Source comparison: historical non-alignment and policy space

The UN record of the Non-Aligned Movement's 1992 Jakarta conference describes members' stated commitments to independent action, development, cooperation and more equitable economic relations. This is a historical primary source presenting the movement's own position; it does not prove members always followed it. UNCTAD has argued that development strategies involve questions of policy space and industrial policy. These are arguments to assess, not neutral guarantees that any one policy succeeds.

Phenomenal World argument card: non-alignment today

Argument to test (teacher paraphrase of Sahay, 9 November 2022)

Sahay argues that developing countries' efforts to preserve room for action should not be treated as proof that they form a unified camp. Their partnerships and priorities differ; bargaining can be issue-specific. This is an interpretation to examine alongside the 1992 movement's own declaration, not a current country ranking.

  1. Does Asteria behave as one predictable bloc actor in the simulation?
  2. What independent evidence would show whether a real government can change partners or terms?

Language bridge

English pathway

Austria connection: ask how Austria, as an EU member and a small open economy, uses EU institutions and trade partnerships; use an official Austrian or EU source for a specific example. Do not equate Austria's institutional position with Asteria's. Original-reading extension: read paragraphs 1 and 2 of the “North–South Dialogue” section in the 1992 Jakarta final document. Paraphrase one goal and identify one historical context that limits direct comparison with today.

Perspective extension: read the opening two paragraphs of Sahay’s “A New Non-Alignment” (9 November 2022). Paraphrase one claim about bargaining, then compare it with one statement in the 1992 Jakarta primary source.

What to hand in

Individual, 400–500 words: assess the negotiated agreement. Include funding arithmetic; one benefit and one dependency; one realistic alternative; analysis of whose voice or cost is missing; and one uncertainty. Success requires distinguishing stated offer from evidence and discussing whether a clause can be enforced.

Exit ticket

  • How can cooperation increase a country's choices and also reduce them?
  • What extra evidence would you need before calling a country non-aligned or autonomous?

After class · up to 25 minutes

In 20 minutes, write a short revision clause for Asteria's agreement. State the trigger, who can request review, what evidence is considered, and what happens if parties disagree. Use the Disruption card and one Position card.
