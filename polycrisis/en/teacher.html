Teacher guide

Preparation, model reasoning and classroom decisions

16 double lessons · Austrian grades 10–12
CourseSyllabusGlossaryToolkitProjectSourcesTeacher guide
Teacher notes and model answers are openly accessible. They are guidance, not a required political conclusion.

This course is a teaching design for a semester elective of 16 sessions, 100 minutes each, for Austrian students aged about 15–18. It offers two language pathways: English-main for bilingual students and German-led with supported English source bridges for students at ordinary Austrian school-English levels. The pathways share the same analytical outcomes and assessment expectations.

Preparation and adaptation

  • Before teaching, check class duration, available weeks, assessment rules, device access and any required school template. The suggested sequence does not establish that the elective is formally approved or determine a school grade conversion.
  • Prepare short source dossiers with dates, authorship, source type, chart notes, and a contrasting view. Supply context for historical cases and make the difference between evidence and interpretation visible.
  • For the English-main pathway, pre-teach a small set of terms and offer German clarification. For the German-led pathway, select brief English excerpts, provide context and a compact glossary, and accept German reasoning and responses.
  • Use classroom simulations only as simplified models. Label invented figures and fictional countries clearly, then debrief which assumptions shaped the result and what the simulation cannot show.
  • Use dated 2022 material as historical analysis. Check current claims and statistics against fresh sources before teaching them; do not present older newsletter essays as up-to-date reporting.

Classroom conditions

  • Do not ask students to disclose family income, energy bills, debts, migration experience or other private household information. Use fictional profiles or public aggregate data for comparisons.
  • Keep political and moral judgements open to disagreement while requiring reasons and evidence. Students may challenge Tooze, Phenomenal World authors, governments, businesses or the polycrisis concept itself.
  • Distinguish a country’s government, residents, firms, workers and organisations; avoid treating a country as a single person with one interest.
  • Check that students can identify the date and limits of data and that charts are not asked to prove more than they show.

Practical delivery

The materials are designed for a static site and can be printed or used on screen. Assignments can be placed in Moodle or another school platform, but the course does not depend on a particular platform, account, upload function or external service. Templates are printable blank tables. Students should keep their work in the format actually assigned by the teacher. Provide reasonable accessible alternatives while preserving the analytical goals and individual accountability.

The rubric is for feedback across five criteria and four levels, 0–3 per criterion. It does not prescribe conversion to Austrian marks. Follow the school’s applicable assessment rules and explain expectations to students before collecting assessed work.

01 · A shopping basket is a world map

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Print or display the reader, four product cards, six mechanism cards, evidence cards, and impact table.
  • Assign each group one product; no student needs to reveal personal shopping or household finances.
  • Remind students that these are mechanisms to investigate; only the stated Eurostat aggregate is a sourced real statistic.

Facilitation

  • Ask groups to explain each arrow aloud before drawing it.
  • When a claim sounds certain, ask: what date, place, measure, and source would establish it?
  • Praise maps that include alternative suppliers or buffers; connections are not inevitably one-way.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • No single map is the answer. A defensible bread chain could be: energy supply shock → some bakeries face higher energy/input costs → a bakery changes prices, margin, recipe, or output → consumers see a possible price or availability effect. Each step needs evidence and the pass-through is not automatically complete.
  • The 62.5% statistic is the 2022 EU energy dependency rate: net imports as a share of gross available energy. It is not Austria’s rate, not a gas-only figure, and not the share of every consumer product imported.
  • Valid unequal-effect examples: a firm with long contracts can be less exposed in the short term; a household with a larger essential-energy budget share may have fewer options. Students should mark these as hypotheses until evidence is supplied.

Watch for

  • A map with many arrows is not automatically a good explanation.
  • A plausible mechanism does not prove that it happened in a particular case.
  • The eventual retail price is influenced by more than one input cost.
  • Imports are not inherently harmful; dependency, alternatives, and bargaining power matter.

Support and stretch

  • Offer a partially completed four-step chain and arrow sentence frames: ‘A may affect B because…’
  • Invite advanced students to add a feedback loop or identify where a buffer can interrupt the chain.
  • Allow diagrams with oral explanation before individual writing; assess reasoning rather than drawing skill.

Extension

Compare the EU aggregate in the supplied Eurostat source with an Austria country figure in the same publication. State the year and definition, then explain why the comparison still cannot predict the price of a chosen product.

02 · When do crises become a polycrisis?

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Display the reader, scenario cards, mechanism cards, test, and audit. Groups need paper or a shared board.
  • Clarify that the scenarios are fictional except where a sourced author’s definition is attributed.
  • Prepare a visible map legend distinguishing supplied facts from hypotheses.

Facilitation

  • Do not reward the biggest map; ask whether each arrow is necessary and supported.
  • Ensure the debate evaluates the idea rather than requiring agreement with Tooze.
  • Invite students to switch sides after the first discussion to separate argument quality from personal preference.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • Card A is simultaneous on supplied information; no interaction is shown. B contains a stated shared bridge mechanism; the claimed health effects still need evidence. C describes a plausible interaction via export receipts and import capacity, but remains fictional and would need data. D is insufficient; it names topics without mechanisms. E shows a buffer against output loss while preserving a cost effect.
  • A polycrisis label is most defensible where distinct problems interact through evidenced mechanisms and the interaction changes consequences or manageability. Reasonable dissent is possible when the evidence does not establish material interaction.
  • An alternative concept might be cascading risk, supply-chain vulnerability, or overlapping crises; students should state why it fits better rather than merely name it.

Watch for

  • Polycrisis does not mean ‘many crises’ by definition.
  • A causal map is an argument, not proof.
  • A feedback loop requires a return path, not just two arrows in one direction.
  • Uncertainty does not mean every claim is equally likely.

Support and stretch

  • Offer node labels and arrow stems for students who need structure.
  • Ask advanced students to distinguish necessary from sufficient conditions or add timing and counterfactuals.
  • Accept oral map defence before written verdict; keep the same intellectual criteria.

Extension

Compare Tooze’s 2022 definition with the 2025 reconsideration. Explain what changed in his use of the term and whether your own test should be revised. Treat this as an author’s evolving interpretation.

03 · Europe’s energy shock, 2021–23

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Print reader, timeline cards, dispatch table, policy cards, and matrix.
  • For the model, write demand = 3 units and explain that units are fictional blocks, not real market volume.
  • Source cards deliberately distinguish yearly from quarterly data and EU totals from country experience.

Facilitation

  • Before connecting events, have students place them on the dated timeline.
  • Ask whether a statement concerns energy supply, wholesale price, retail bill, or public spending.
  • During policy comparison, insist that a response can have both beneficiaries and costs; do not imply every trade-off is equal in scale.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • Timeline order: 2021 demand recovery/tight market and 44% Russian share; 24 February 2022 invasion; 2022 annual import dependency 62.5%; August demand-reduction regulation; Q4 Russian pipeline share around 15%; 2023 annual dependence reported as 15%. The cards mix annual and quarterly measures, which students must state.
  • Merit-order model baseline dispatch: wind 1, nuclear 1, gas 1; marginal gas price 60 €/MWh. With gas at 100, the dispatch remains wind 1, nuclear 1, gas 1, marginal price 100 €/MWh. In this stylised model all 3 units clear at that price. Limits: real bids and contracts differ; demand, networks, market coupling, time intervals and regulations matter; offered price is not the same as a household bill.
  • A valid chain: reduced pipeline deliveries amid demand and geopolitical uncertainty → tighter expected supply / higher gas prices (needs market evidence) → possible higher wholesale electricity prices in hours with gas marginal (model explains mechanism, not historical attribution) → governments pursue substitute supply, demand reduction, or support. Students should not state the model proves exact historical pass-through.
  • Response assessments are open-ended if benefit, cost, and evidence question are coherent. Example: LNG may help replace pipeline supply in the short run, while infrastructure and global price competition create costs; benefits and impacts vary by place and timing.

Watch for

  • The full-scale invasion began in February 2022; energy prices had already risen in 2021.
  • A 15% EU demand target was not a guaranteed 15% cut by every household.
  • A wholesale price is not identical to a retail bill.
  • EU-wide import shares are not the share for every member state.
  • The fictional market calculation demonstrates a mechanism, not the historical price level.

Support and stretch

  • Provide a timeline with dates already ordered; ask students to annotate mechanisms instead.
  • Advanced students compare annual and quarterly denominators or explain how contracts can delay retail pass-through.
  • Give a calculation scaffold: fill cheapest unit first, track cumulative supply, then identify the last unit.

Extension

Investigate why electricity prices can move with gas prices even when not all electricity is generated from gas. Use the Commission’s explanation of market operation and distinguish a mechanism from a claim about any specific hour.

04 · Inflation: what does the average measure?

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Print the reader, arithmetic check, fictional tables, Austria evidence card, and response cards.
  • Emphasise historical data: 8.6% is the 2022 annual average VPI; 10.5% is December 2022 year-on-year HICP.
  • No student should be asked to share household budget details.

Facilitation

  • Have students state the period before interpreting any percentage.
  • Distinguish percentage points from percent if students compare rates.
  • When discussing policy, separate empirical claims about effects from value judgements about fairness or priorities.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • Quick check: 8.6%; 108.6 is the index level, with 8.6% change; prices still rose at a slower rate when inflation fell 8%→3%; €2.00→€2.20 is a 10% increase.
  • Fictional basket total: A = 10×€2 + 20×€1 + 1×€40 = €80. B = 10×€2.20 + 20×€1.05 + 1×€44 = €87. Percentage change = (€87−€80) ÷ €80 × 100 = 8.75%. Item price changes: bread 10%, bus tickets 5%, jacket 10%. Weighted contributions are 25%×10%=2.5 percentage points; 25%×5%=1.25 points; 50%×10%=5 points. Sum = 8.75%, matching the fixed-quantity basket calculation.
  • Fictional category-weighted household exercise: headline = 0.30×12 + 0.20×10 + 0.15×4 + 0.35×3 = 7.25%; Pine = 5.4 + 2.5 + 0.4 + 0.6 = 8.9%; River = 2.4 + 1.5 + 1.0 + 1.2 = 6.1%. Households differ because category weights differ. All figures are fictional.
  • Statistik Austria VPI: annual-average 2022 vs annual-average 2021, 8.6%; 2021 was 2.8%. Eurostat HICP: Austria December 2022 vs December 2021, 10.5%; EU 10.4%. Different index construction and comparison period mean the figures answer different questions.
  • A reasonable policy answer identifies target, timing, payer, possible behavioural effects, implementation, and evidence. No single option is automatically fair or effective in every context.

Watch for

  • Inflation falling does not mean prices fell; it means the rate of increase slowed.
  • An index value is not itself the inflation rate.
  • An average index does not describe every household, but it remains useful for a defined question.
  • 8.6% and 10.5% are not conflicting readings of the same measure and period.
  • Basket weights should correspond to spending shares; do not average item rates without weights.

Support and stretch

  • Provide the percentage-change formula and an example with each step filled in.
  • Advanced students explain how changing weights, substitution, housing treatment, and timing affect comparisons, using the methodological source.
  • Let students annotate a completed example before calculating independently; assess interpretation as well as arithmetic.

Extension

Read Statistik Austria’s detailed 2022 release and identify the categories contributing most to the annual change. Compare contribution to inflation with percentage price change; they are related but not identical.

05 · Who should be protected when energy prices rise?

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Print or display all material cards and worksheets; assign roles so each group can hear different interests.
  • Remind students that the three household profiles and the 40% price rise are fictional teaching data, not Austrian measurements.
  • If using the linked Austrian report in class, select one chart in advance and check its definition, population and period.

Facilitation

  • Keep the hearing focused on mechanisms and distribution: ask who receives money, who pays, how fast it works, and what changes energy use.
  • Require groups to identify whether a statement is directly evidenced, inferred, or a value judgement.
  • Invite students to combine tools only if they identify funding and explain implementation.
  • Do not ask students to reveal family income, bills, housing quality or energy insecurity.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • New monthly bills: A €210, B €336, C €420. Shares of monthly income: A 14%; B 7%; C about 15.6%. Calculation: old bill × 1.40; divide by monthly income × 100. The fictional data suggest C has the highest burden share, but do not establish all vulnerability dimensions.
  • There is no single correct recommendation. A defensible answer may favour targeted support plus efficiency investment, explaining eligibility, delivery time, financing and protection for renters. A price cap may also be defended if the student addresses fiscal cost, scope and conservation incentives.
  • Evidence card claim: the Commission reported a package of measures and judged that these contributed alongside other measures. It does not establish a single causal effect.

Watch for

  • A falling inflation rate means prices have returned to their old level; it usually means prices are rising more slowly.
  • A price cap makes energy costless; somebody bears the difference or supply/investment may be affected.
  • An efficiency grant automatically helps renters; the owner may control the building work.
  • A household's bill alone measures hardship; income, household needs, building conditions and ability to adjust matter.

Support and stretch

  • Offer sentence stems: “This helps ___ because ___”; “The cost may fall on ___”; “The evidence shows ___ but does not show ___.”
  • For extension, ask students to design eligibility criteria and test who is missed, or compare universal and targeted support using the fictional households.
  • Allow a student to act as rapporteur or evidence checker while still completing the individual memo.

Extension

Compare one Austrian energy-support instrument with the three classroom tools. Use an official Austrian source, identify its date and eligibility rules, then assess one distributional consequence. Treat this as a source investigation, not as proof of effectiveness.

06 · When the world competes for gas

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Prepare three region cards, tokens or paper counters, auction record sheets, and a visible timer.
  • Appoint auctioneer and timekeeper; if the class is large, make subteams within each region that must agree on one bid.
  • Stress that the regions, quantities, budgets and points are all fictional and do not represent actual states.

Facilitation

  • Do not force the auction toward a predetermined result. Let budgets and bids determine allocation under stated rules.
  • If students make a mathematically impossible bid, explain why and allow a correction before revealing bids.
  • Keep debrief questions on the model's mechanism and limits; avoid claims that the game predicts real LNG prices.
  • Ask who is missing from the model: consumers within regions, exporters, workers, climate effects, and people without a voice in government.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • Worked example for a possible Round 1: if A bids 5 tokens/unit, B 3, C 1, A can win up to 5 units for 25 tokens; the next four units can be won by B at 3 each, spending 12, leaving C with no units. This is only one possible result; bids are team choices.
  • If the Round 2 agreement reserves two units for B and C, each receives one at 1 token. The remaining six units are auctioned. A transfer can change what a team can bid; a price ceiling may lead to pass/shortage if bids exceed 2. Teams should calculate actual outcomes from their table.
  • Scoring: service points = 4 × units received (up to need); hardship penalty = 3 × unmet units; net model points = service points − tokens spent. Explain that this formula is intentionally crude and not a valuation of wellbeing.
  • A fairer rule may reserve essential-use quantities, use needs-based allocation, or combine a price auction with a safety floor. Each has trade-offs, such as weaker price signals, disputes about measuring need, or administration.

Watch for

  • LNG is instantly available wherever a buyer wants it; liquefaction, shipping and terminal capacity constrain delivery.
  • A higher price always means greater social need; it may instead reflect greater ability to pay.
  • The simulation proves European LNG buying caused shortages elsewhere; it models a mechanism but does not establish historical causation.
  • Cooperation has no cost; reserved supply, transfers and price limits distribute costs and can affect incentives.

Support and stretch

  • Provide a worked bid example: budget 9 tokens, desire 3 units, maximum affordable average is 3 tokens per unit; remind teams a total bid cannot exceed budget.
  • Offer a recorder role and a bid calculator role; all students contribute to the individual explanation.
  • Extension: ask students to add storage or a long-term contract rule and predict how it changes bargaining power.

Extension

Compare the game with one dated official account of a real energy-market response. Identify one mechanism the game captures and at least two real mechanisms it omits. Do not infer a historical causal effect from the game.

07 · Sri Lanka 2022: reconstructing a crisis

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Print source/event cards and worksheets; optionally colour-code shock, vulnerability, decision and outcome cards.
  • Prepare a neutral map of Sri Lanka and a simple timeline from 2019 to 2022.
  • Use the supplied source dates: the February 2022 Article IV analysis predates the full crisis; the March 2023 reports are retrospective and connected to an IMF programme.

Facilitation

  • Insist that students include domestic decisions alongside global shocks and prior vulnerabilities.
  • Explain that causal contribution is not the same as sole cause, and policy choices are not the same as inevitable outcomes.
  • Compare source roles: IMF staff analysis, an executive director's statement, and a World Bank press release. Do not declare one source neutral by label alone.
  • Ask students to distinguish reported event, source interpretation, and their own causal inference.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • Chronology: Easter attacks 2019; tax cuts late 2019; pandemic 2020 onward; fertiliser ban 2021; managed/fixed exchange rate and reserve pressures; Ukraine invasion and higher commodity prices from 2022; sharp depreciation and debt-service suspension in April 2022; shortages, hardship and protests during 2022. Processes overlap; cards are not a complete history.
  • A defensible chain: tax cuts reduced revenue; pandemic weakened tourism and activity; deficits and borrowing needs rose while market access was constrained; debt payments and import needs used foreign currency; limited reserves made import financing harder; global fuel/food price rises raised the cost of essential imports; depreciation increased rupee costs and local-currency value of foreign-currency debt; shortages and power cuts harmed activity and households.
  • Domestic decisions to include: the 2019 tax cuts, exchange-rate/reserve strategy, monetary financing/low rates, pricing policies, chemical-fertiliser ban. Students should not claim each had equal effect; the provided sources vary in diagnosis.
  • IMF staff and executive director statements overlap on shocks and policy mistakes but differ in emphases. The executive director statement foregrounds missteps. It is valid to ask for independent data and other perspectives; the statement alone cannot quantify each policy's causal contribution.

Watch for

  • Sri Lanka's crisis was caused only by the Ukraine war, COVID-19, or one domestic policy; multiple causes interacted over time.
  • Debt default means all debt vanished; it means payments were suspended or missed and negotiations followed.
  • A currency depreciation only helps exporters; it also raises local-currency import costs and foreign-currency debt burdens.
  • An official report is automatically neutral; institutional role, timing and purpose matter alongside evidence.

Support and stretch

  • Offer a partially completed timeline with dates while requiring students to explain arrows themselves.
  • Provide mechanism stems: “This reduced ___, which made ___ harder because …”; “The source reports …; I infer …”.
  • Extension: compare how the sequence might differ if reserves were larger, while clearly labelling this as a counterfactual rather than fact.

Extension

Read the full 2023 IMF report's debt sustainability annex and one independent academic or investigative account. Compare the treatment of creditor structure and domestic policy; keep IMF recommendations distinct from historical description.

08 · Debt, dollars, and room to act

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Print budget and role cards; provide calculators or allow written arithmetic.
  • Before teaching, note that Country L's budget and response effects are fictional; the historical Sri Lanka figures are dated and attributed to the IMF report.
  • Budget units: 1 LU equals 1 billion local-currency units; the FX payment is US$100 million, which costs 30 LU at 300 local-currency units per dollar. Check that Scenario A totals 130 LU (30 LU gap) and Scenario B totals 135 LU (35 LU gap).

Facilitation

  • Have students show units in conversions: US dollars × local units per dollar = local units.
  • Keep reserves distinct from government revenue and distinguish liquidity (paying now) from solvency (whether debt can be paid over time).
  • Prompt students to ask whose spending is reduced and who receives protection under each cabinet package.
  • Treat IMF recommendations as the institution's policy view, separate from dates and figures in the report.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • Currency conversions: US$100m × 300 local units/US$ = 30,000m local units = 30 LU when 1 LU = 1 billion local units; ×400 = 40 LU; rise = (40−30)/30 = 33.3%. US$250m ×400 = 100 LU.
  • Budget: planned local spending = 45 + 20 + 10 + 10 = 85 LU; FX debt is US$100m ×300 = 30 LU, giving planned total 115 and gap 15. Scenario A at 450: FX payment = 45 LU, total 130 and gap 30. Scenario B import bill = 25 LU, other spending 90; FX debt 45; total 135 and gap 35. For the cabinet package, restructuring can reduce the 45 LU FX payment by up to 22.5 LU; a bridge loan covers up to 8 LU. Together they cover 30.5 of the 35 LU Scenario B gap, leaving 4.5 LU. Real macroeconomic feedbacks are excluded.
  • Sri Lanka card facts: usable reserves US$1.6bn at end-2021 and described as under one month of imports; debt-service suspension announced 12 April 2022; ISB default date 18 May 2022; debt ~128% GDP in 2022 per 2023 IMF staff report. Keep dates and attribution attached.
  • Option discussion: reserves provide immediate FX but shrink a buffer; new loans bridge payments but increase future obligations; restructuring reduces or delays payments but redistributes losses and takes negotiation; spending cuts and taxes have distributional effects. No universal best answer.

Watch for

  • A depreciation automatically increases the number of local currency units needed for every foreign-currency payment; it does, holding the dollar amount fixed and using the stated quote.
  • A government can print dollars to pay dollar debt; it generally needs foreign currency from earnings, borrowing, reserves or other inflows.
  • Default means a government ceases to exist or owes nothing; it means obligations were not paid on schedule and negotiations may follow.
  • Reserves and revenue are the same; reserves are foreign assets/buffers, while revenue is a flow of income to government.
  • The fictional budget predicts a real crisis; it isolates arithmetic while excluding behavioural and macroeconomic feedbacks.

Support and stretch

  • Provide a conversion frame and one worked line; extension students calculate percentage changes and explain the quote convention.
  • Offer role cards as optional viewpoints while each student still writes the independent memo.
  • Extension: add a feedback loop in which depreciation raises import costs and inflation, then discuss what evidence would be needed to substantiate it.

Extension

Compare Sri Lanka's foreign-currency exposure with a country borrowing mainly in its own currency, using dated official debt data. Explain why currency denomination alone does not determine debt sustainability; include maturity, interest, revenue, reserves and institutional context.

09 · After the disaster: who pays for recovery?

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Print or display all Luma materials; assign the four role cards.
  • Write the key calculation: 120 − 25 = 95; no real case figures are used.
  • Open the two UNFCCC sources and select the official outcome section for the short comparison.

Facilitation

  • Keep claims about climate attribution separate from the fictional storm scenario: this case does not attribute a particular event.
  • Insist students state whether a link is given, assumed or uncertain.
  • Allow a deadlocked negotiation; assess the reasoning and record the gap rather than inventing a solution.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • Starting financing gap = LC 95m. Grant + loan = 80m, remaining gap 15m; grant + loan + pause = 100m, leaving LC 5m after the gap (LC 5m surplus relative to urgent estimate), before any conditions are evaluated. Grant + pause = 50m, leaving LC 45m.
  • Debt-service pause frees cash now but does not cancel the liability. The loan brings LC 50m now but LC 60m due in five years. Reserve use has an opportunity cost.
  • Good causal chains include port damage → delayed imports/exports → pressure on foreign currency; or damage → repair spending → financing need. They should be expressed as possibilities where data are absent.

Watch for

  • A climate disaster is not proof that climate change caused this specific event.
  • A balanced budget is not proof that needs are fully counted.
  • Debt relief, a payment pause and a grant are different instruments.

Support and stretch

  • Offer a partially completed causal map and sentence frames; extension students add a distributional effect and compare two source genres.
  • Students may submit the negotiation calculation orally before writing the individual judgement.

Extension

Compare the UNFCCC loss-and-damage fund with adaptation finance: what problem is each intended to address, and where can purposes overlap? Use official UNFCCC sources and cite the date.

10 · Who owns the transition? Energy, jobs and place

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Display or print all cards and table; prepare one Shock card to draw.
  • Score starting values by summing the selected project effects; track any unmet prerequisite as zero effect.
  • Open Commission sources; explain that policy instruments and eligibility are more complex than the simulation.

Facilitation

  • Ensure students do not treat public, private or cooperative ownership as automatically equitable or efficient.
  • Check the budget and each project prerequisite before voting.
  • Ask which absent groups (for example, future residents or landowners) might change the decision.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • A valid example: grid/wind-solar (35) + insulation/heat pumps (30) + worker training (25) + cooperative solar (20) = 110, over budget and invalid. A valid plan: insulation (30) + training (25) + district heat (40) + bill support (15) = 110 also invalid. Students must select within 100; e.g. insulation (30) + training (25) + cooperative solar (20) + bill support (15) = 90: climate 4, worker 3, household protection 4, reliability 0, so it fails reliability minimum. Add grid connection (35) would exceed budget. This exposes a real planning conflict; an example passing plan: district heat (40) + worker training (25) + cooperative solar (20) + bill support (15) = 100; climate 4, reliability 3, worker 3, household 3, with renter-access prerequisite for solar and placement plan for training. However district heat also requires 5 PU maintenance reserve, making total 105; thus that package is invalid unless bill support is dropped (85 projects + 5 reserve = 90) and thresholds still household protection 1, yes, climate 4/reliability 3/worker 3/household 1. Apply shock and discuss.
  • Scoring has a deliberate budget tension; count the 5 PU district-heat reserve as a cost. There is at least one feasible package: district heat 40 + training 25 + cooperative solar 20 + bill support 15 + reserve 5 = 105 (not feasible). District heat 40 + insulation 30 + worker training 25 = 95 plus reserve 5 =100: climate 4, reliability 2, workers 3, household 2; meets thresholds if renter access and placement plan are secured.
  • After Shock card, different plans lose different dimensions; there is no single best policy without value choices.

Watch for

  • A new renewable project does not automatically guarantee reliable supply; grids and timing matter.
  • Jobs are not interchangeable just because totals match; skills, location, pay and security matter.
  • Ownership form alone does not settle accountability or distribution.

Support and stretch

  • Provide a score tracker with thresholds printed; extension students critique the chosen indicators and add a missing stakeholder.
  • For language support, allow oral negotiation notes before the individual written submission.

Extension

Use an official Austrian regional or Just Transition Fund project source to test one assumption in the game; record what the source measures and what it does not.

11 · Batteries: who captures the value?

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Print chain cards, fictional sales table and term menu.
  • Open IEA report; use 2025 report as a dated source, not latest market data.
  • Check arithmetic: sales shares are 8%, 20%, 24%, 30%, 18%.

Facilitation

  • Make clear all Kalo data and company terms are invented.
  • Distinguish gross sales, value added, profit, tax and wages.
  • Do not equate mineral abundance with automatic bargaining power or development.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • One valid deal: accept water monitoring (mandatory; resident trust +2), training (workers +2 skills; company cost −1), and technology partnership (workers +1, firms +1; company control −1). Combined company penalties are cost 1 and control 1, within limits; government approval and worker/resident support criteria are met. Another valid deal can combine monitoring, local suppliers and training: company cost penalties total 2; firm capability +2, worker skills +2, trust +2. A deal with monitoring + tax floor + local supplier has cost penalties 3 and the consortium must reject or renegotiate. These are examples, not the only defensible outcomes.
  • Fictional gross-sales shares: 8/100=8%; 20%; 24%; 30%; 18%. They sum to 100, but intermediate transactions are repeatedly counted; they are not profit shares or national value added.
  • IEA official findings support the idea that concentration and shocks can create vulnerability and that chemistry changes affect input chains. These global patterns do not establish Kalo's project viability.

Watch for

  • Lithium extraction is only one stage; refining, components, cells and systems can matter.
  • A new factory can create jobs without creating local technology ownership or supplier links.
  • Recycling can contribute materials but does not remove all near-term supply needs.

Support and stretch

  • Provide a stage strip with arrows for support; extension students compare two chemistries or design a compliance indicator.
  • Allow group calculation rehearsal, then require each student to show their own share calculation in submission.

Extension

Compare the IEA global map with one specific Austrian company or official investment announcement. Ask what information about ownership, domestic value added and public support is disclosed.

12 · Cooperate, compete, or stay flexible?

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Print offer table, position cards and rules; assign four negotiating roles plus observer.
  • Check funding arithmetic: bank grant 25 plus Asteria minimum 10 = 35; the rest must be covered. North loan 60 plus public 20 can reach 80; South investment 40 plus bank 25 plus public 15 can reach 80 if conditions compatible.
  • Open the UN 1992 document and UNCTAD 2014 press page; date the claims clearly.

Facilitation

  • Avoid presenting non-alignment as a timeless label with a single definition.
  • Distinguish actors' claims from official source evidence; ask whose perspective an official source represents.
  • Keep autonomy concrete: ability to change supplier, revise policy, finance alternatives and enforce terms.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • North grid loan 60 + Asteria public budget 20 = 80: valid, with repayment 72 plus 12-year service contract and procurement preference. If North shortens the service contract to 8 years, Asteria must add 5 units for training, giving 60 + 25 = 85. The Bank grant 25 + maximum public 25 = 50, so the remaining grid gap is 30. South’s 40 private plant investment is never grid cash; it may form a separate deal with its own mineral-supply/control obligations. Bank open procurement and North preference conflict unless renegotiated.
  • Grant, loan and private investment are different. Private investment is not public aid; a loan is cash now and repayment later; a grant need not be repaid but conditions apply.
  • The 1992 NAM document is a primary statement of declared principles; assessment of practice needs concrete decisions and independent evidence.

Watch for

  • Non-alignment does not mean no partnerships or automatic neutrality.
  • More partners do not automatically mean more autonomy; multiple obligations can compound.
  • Policy space is constrained by contracts, finance, institutions and domestic choices, not only by formal treaties.

Support and stretch

  • Provide a priority-ranking frame and arithmetic scaffold; extension students analyze the tension between procurement rules and an exclusive partner clause.
  • Allow a short oral negotiation role, while each student completes the written individual assessment.

Extension

Choose a contemporary official agreement or financing announcement and examine its clauses for review, exit, procurement, local training and data disclosure. Treat promotional claims as claims; seek the signed document or independent evidence.

13 · Oil shocks then and now

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Print or project the case reader, evidence cards, matrix, response frame, and feedback check.
  • Place students in pairs for stations, then groups of three; keep the break at five minutes.
  • Clarify that card figures are historical estimates or institutional summaries, each scoped as stated.

Facilitation

  • Insist that students explain causal arrows; proximity in time is not proof of causation.
  • When students say ‘the 1970s crisis’, ask which episode and whose experience they mean.
  • Use Portfolio 3 as an individual assessment even if the map is collaborative.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • A strong comparison could identify import dependence, limited short-run substitution, and price transmission as recurring mechanisms, while distinguishing oil embargo/production cuts from the later gas and LNG-centered European shock.
  • The IEA attributes the recent tight market to several factors and identifies the invasion as a major escalation; do not reduce this to one cause.
  • Card D reports EU energy consumption overall, not a claim that exactly one-quarter of every member state's supply was Russian.

Watch for

  • OAPEC and OPEC are interchangeable: OAPEC is the Arab producers’ organization; keep the source’s exact name.
  • A price rise means physical supply stopped everywhere: prices can change due to expected scarcity and competition even when fuel continues to flow.
  • A comparison proves policy transfer: a repeated mechanism does not guarantee the same outcome or solution.

Support and stretch

  • Offer sentence frames and partially completed matrix rows while keeping the same claim/evidence/mechanism expectations.
  • For extension, ask students to investigate whether energy intensity changed after the 1970s, with a dated national series and careful unit comparison.

Extension

Compare the IEA's account with the contemporary US diplomatic record. Ask whose perspective each source makes visible and which affected groups remain absent.

14 · Project workshop: make the question smaller

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Print route cards, narrowing ladder, question tests, source cards, log, planner, and checkpoint card.
  • Prepare accessible links or offline copies of the public data pages; confirm classroom internet works.
  • Remind students that common glossary, source log, and project rubric/planner are course-wide materials.

Facilitation

  • Approve questions by feasibility, not by whether they support a favoured policy.
  • Require a specific population/time/place and a credible evidence trail before students draft.
  • Keep pair planning accountable: every student keeps an individual source log and prepares for an individual oral defence.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • A strong question identifies a bounded case, a mechanism, a time period, and available evidence; there is no single correct topic.
  • Data sources establish what their defined measure records; they rarely establish the full causal story alone.
  • A contrasting analysis need not be artificially equal in evidential weight; students should assess quality and relevance.

Watch for

  • More sources automatically mean a better project: relevance and source quality matter.
  • A chart proves causation: charts describe patterns; mechanisms and alternative explanations require further evidence.
  • A government/country is one unified actor: institutions, firms, households, workers, and communities can have different interests.

Support and stretch

  • Offer a choice among the four prepared routes, a question frame, and preselected open-access data links.
  • Extend by asking students to compare indicators or explain why a proposed source cannot answer their question.

Extension

Students may prepare a short prospectus for teacher approval, including one sentence explaining why the polycrisis concept is useful for this exact question.

15 · Challenge the concept: when does ‘polycrisis’ help?

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Print reader, argument cards, classification cards, map, peer roles, critique protocol, feedback form, revision table, and oral prompts.
  • Arrange 24 learners as eight triads; use one 2-minute presentation and concise feedback cycles per student, or pairs if needed.
  • Read Tooze 407 in advance; present it as a contestable essay, not official course doctrine.

Facilitation

  • Keep attention on reasoning and evidence, not on voting for or against a label.
  • Ensure critique does not become a demand for false balance or a claim that all interpretations are equally supported.
  • If a student discusses a specific harm, use precise language and avoid replacing named actors or events with an abstract ‘crisis’.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • The strongest answer may be conditional: the lens helps where it exposes evidenced interaction; it hinders where it obscures mechanisms, actors, perspective, or specific harm.
  • Tooze’s essay retains multiple explanatory approaches and warns against one universal method; do not simplify it as a complete rejection of polycrisis.
  • Statement ‘two events happened together, so one caused the other’ is an unsupported inference unless additional evidence establishes a mechanism.

Watch for

  • Critiquing the concept requires rejecting it everywhere.
  • Naming agency means ignoring structural constraints, or vice versa.
  • Being fair means treating every claim as equally evidenced.

Support and stretch

  • Offer oral sentence frames and let a learner present a diagram rather than read prose; keep evidence challenges.
  • Extend by comparing the 2022 framing and 2025 essay directly and asking what changed in the object being described and in the author's vantage point.

Extension

Ask students to draft a short ‘when to use / when not to use’ rule for the class glossary, supported by one example and one counterexample.

16 · Student Chartbook conference

Student lesson · Printable worksheet

Preparation

  • Arrange six groups of four for the conference; normally each group contains two project pairs. Prepare each pair's two-minute presentation order.
  • With one teacher, conduct individual defences sequentially: 24 students × 100 seconds = 40 minutes. Use a visible timer and prepared question cards; students waiting complete the supplied independent chart/source audit. For a larger class, continue defences next lesson. For accessibility needs, arrange an individual alternative response to be assessed later; do not assume another teacher is available.
  • Print conference checklist, audience cards, oral cards, chart/source audit, synthesis board, reflection and exit slips. Check final drafts against the common course rubric/planner.

Facilitation

  • Keep the presentation schedule visible and enforce kind, evidence-focused questions.
  • For paired projects, ask each learner a different individual defence question; each must know the project as a whole.
  • Assess subject reasoning and English fluency separately where language development is an explicit course aim.

Possible answers and worked examples

  • There is no single correct project conclusion. Strong answers identify the evidence's scope and unit, explain one mechanism, and identify a limit.
  • Students may conclude the polycrisis concept is useful, partly useful, or unhelpful, if their reasoning fits the case and evidence.
  • An uncertainty is not a failure; unsupported certainty is a weakness.
  • For 24 learners, a sequential teacher-heard defence is feasible at 100 seconds each (two 40-second answers plus 20 seconds for notes/transition); the independent audit keeps waiting students on a supplied task.

Watch for

  • A visually attractive chart is automatically valid: check axes, definitions, dates, and source.
  • A project with four sources is automatically balanced: source type, independence, relevance, and method matter.
  • Oral defence is a memory test: it should probe reasoning and evidence, with a source card available.

Support and stretch

  • Offer presenter notes with keywords, advance access to question cards, and an alternate presentation format where needed.
  • Extension: ask students to propose one follow-up study design, identifying a new dataset and what causal claim it could test.

Extension

Compile the synthesis board into a class Chartbook using only student-approved project titles and public sources. Keep it as a classroom artifact unless students and teacher choose otherwise.
