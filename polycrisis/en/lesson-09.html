Teacher notes

Learning goals

A storm or flood is a physical event. Its effects depend on where people live, what infrastructure exists, how the state raises money, and whether recovery funds arrive as grants or loans. Today we trace those connections and negotiate a recovery plan for a fictional island state.

  • Explain how physical damage can affect public budgets, imports, jobs and debt.
  • Distinguish grants, loans and delayed debt payments, including who bears each cost.
  • Use evidence and uncertainty to defend a recovery package.

Your 100-minute lesson

  1. 10 min

    Map the shock

    On the Cause-chain worksheet: connect hazard, damage, income, imports and public finance; circle one uncertain link. Hand in your first causal map.

  2. 15 min

    Sort the evidence

    Use Evidence cards: Luma and Source comparison: Loss and Damage Fund. Mark each claim FACT, ASSUMPTION or VALUE; deliver a marked evidence table and one limit.

  3. 15 min

    Calculate the funding gap

    Use Luma budget table and Budget worksheet. Calculate the gap for each offer combination; deliver your arithmetic and whether urgent costs are covered.

  4. 20 min

    Negotiate recovery

    Follow Complete Recovery negotiation rules and role cards. Record the agreement on Distribution cards and signed recovery terms; deliver the completed record and a three-point justification.

  5. 5 min

    Break

    Take a five-minute break.

  6. 15 min

    Audit who bears the cost

    Use Distribution cards and signed recovery terms. Trace who pays now, who repays later and who may be left out. Deliver a revised causal map with winners and risks.

  7. 10 min

    Compare with real institutions

    Read Source comparison: Loss and Damage Fund and Phenomenal World argument card: climate finance. Compare the UNFCCC description and the argument with one fictional rule; state one similarity, difference and missing assumption.

  8. 10 min

    Individual judgement

    Use Exit and Submission prompt. Write the individual recovery judgement with one calculation, one evidence-based mechanism and one limitation.

Materials

Case reader: Luma after Cyclone Nera

Fictional case reader (teacher-written; about 300 words)

Luma is a fictional island country with 1.2 million people. A powerful cyclone crosses its eastern coast. The wind damages homes and a port; floodwater ruins roads, a hospital, and part of the rice harvest. No single event explains the losses. Families living in stronger houses recover sooner. A damaged bridge cuts off a rural district, while the port disruption delays fuel and medicine. Some shops close; workers lose wages. These links are plausible mechanisms in this exercise, not measured facts about a real country.

The government estimates urgent public repairs at 120 million Luma credits (LC). It has 25 million LC available after protecting ordinary health and education spending. The remaining 95 million LC is a financing gap. The estimate covers public facilities and emergency support; it does not price every private loss, ecosystem loss, or long-term health effect. Students should therefore avoid treating the number as the full cost of the disaster.

Luma earns foreign currency by selling tea and fish and by welcoming visitors. The cyclone damages the port and several hotels just before the main visitor season. If export and visitor receipts fall, Luma may have less foreign currency to buy imported fuel, medicines, construction material and food. This is a possible second-round effect. It depends on how long facilities remain closed, whether buyers switch suppliers, and whether visitors return. A recovery plan that repairs the port first might restart earnings sooner, but people without safe homes may reasonably argue that housing and clinics must come first.

Three kinds of support are on the table: grants that do not require repayment; loans that must be repaid; and a temporary pause in existing debt payments. A loan can provide money quickly but adds future obligations. A pause frees cash today but does not erase the debt. A grant avoids repayment but may be limited or slow. Decisions also involve fairness: should funders give more because they have greater resources, because they contributed more to emissions, or because Luma has greater need? Those are political and ethical arguments, not calculations that this fictional budget can settle. The class will negotiate a package, then test its consequences and limits.

Evidence cards: Luma

Cut or assign one card per group

  • Card A — Repair estimate: public repairs and emergency support are estimated at LC 120 million. This is a fictional case input, not a real assessment.
  • Card B — Available funds: LC 25 million is available without reducing the protected health and education budgets. Fictional input.
  • Card C — Port: the port is damaged; fuel and medicine deliveries may be delayed. The length of delay is unknown.
  • Card D — Foreign currency: tea, fish and tourism normally bring in foreign currency. How much income the cyclone disrupts is unknown.
  • Card E — Unequal exposure: households in weaker housing and isolated villages may face greater risks. This is a scenario assumption; local data would be needed to measure it.
  • Card F — Emissions and responsibility: the exercise supplies no emissions data for Luma or other states. Do not claim the case proves who caused this cyclone.

Complete Recovery negotiation rules and role cards

Set-up and rules (20 minutes)

  1. Form four groups and assign one role each. Each group reads its card silently for 2 minutes, then names its priority and one condition it can accept.
  2. There are two 6-minute negotiation rounds and a 4-minute drafting period. Groups may make written offers using only the Funding menu. One offer may be conditional.
  3. To pass, at least three of four roles must sign, including every provider whose grant, loan or pause is counted. The package must cover LC 95m or name a specific, openly accepted unfunded amount. A provider can refuse to sign; its offer then cannot be counted. No facilitator invents extra money.
  4. Record each offer, condition, signature and any remaining gap. If no agreement passes, the class records the most-supported proposal and the unresolved gap; this is a valid outcome.

Role cards

  • Luma government: You must protect emergency services and want to rebuild the port. You will accept up to LC 10m in reserve use. You oppose a loan unless its terms include a realistic repayment plan. Ask for grant support first.
  • Rural residents and local workers: You want safe homes, clinic access and jobs for local workers. You can accept port spending if the plan also funds a rural road and publishes a worker-hiring target. You have no authority to promise government money.
  • Regional Bank: You offer the LC 50m recovery loan only if at least LC 20m goes to port and road repairs. You may agree to a 12-month debt-service pause on existing payments, freeing LC 20m now; the old debt remains. You cannot convert the loan into a grant.
  • Relief Coalition: You control the LC 30m grant. You want transparent spending and priority for urgent services. You may split it between housing/clinics and infrastructure, but total grant cannot exceed LC 30m.

Debrief

Which rule gave a role power? Did a mathematically balanced package distribute risk fairly? How would the outcome change if repair estimates were wrong, money arrived late, or losses continued? The game compresses real negotiations, excludes many affected voices, assumes fixed offers, and cannot model trust or long-term economic change.

Source comparison: Loss and Damage Fund

The UNFCCC describes the Fund for responding to Loss and Damage as part of new funding arrangements established under UN climate negotiations. COP28 operationalized the Fund through a decision. The official decision and Fund materials explain governance and access arrangements; they do not make the fictional LC offers in this classroom game actual Fund terms.

Distribution cards and signed recovery terms

Distribution cards

  • Borrowers: future taxpayers may repay the LC 60m loan; ask which services could compete for those funds.
  • Residents without safe housing: may benefit if grants go to shelter and clinics; may be missed if funds prioritize the port.
  • Exporters and importers: may benefit from a repaired port; may still face delays or currency shortages.
  • Rural workers: may gain from road repair and local hiring; the agreement has no guaranteed job count.
  • Future residents: inherit repaired infrastructure and remaining debt; long-term effects are unknown.

Exit and Submission prompt

Individual submission (350–450 words)

Recommend a Luma recovery package. Include: (1) the funding-gap calculation; (2) two causal links; (3) who bears one cost or risk; (4) one finding from an official source; (5) one uncertainty or limitation. Show arithmetic. Separate evidence from values and acknowledge an alternative.

Exit questions

  1. Which funding choice changes Luma’s future obligations, and how?
  2. Name one connection in your causal map that needs more evidence.

Phenomenal World argument card: climate finance

Argument to test (teacher paraphrase of Mackenzie and Sahay, 18 November 2022)

Mackenzie and Sahay argue that climate finance is shaped by domestic political interests and financial constraints, not only by a lack of cooperation between countries. A climate plan can be limited by who controls investment and by pressure to protect existing returns. This is an argument, not a measured description of Luma.

  1. Which part of this argument appears in the Luma simulation, and which is missing?
  2. What official evidence would help test it in a real case?

Language bridge

English pathway

Connect the fictional island case to Austria: use Statistik Austria's CPI or an Austrian flood-risk map as a comparison of how prices or exposure are measured. Do not imply that Austria and Luma face the same hazard or financing constraints. Original-reading extension: read the UNFCCC COP28 outcome page; inspect the decision title and the first operative section on establishing/operationalizing arrangements. Paraphrase one institutional rule in your own words and compare it with a Luma rule.

Perspective extension: read the opening two paragraphs of Mackenzie and Sahay’s “Collective Action and Climate Finance” (18 November 2022). Paraphrase their explanation of climate-finance constraints; test one part against the UNFCCC source and name one simulation assumption.
