Challenge the concept: when does ‘polycrisis’ help?

Does the polycrisis concept clarify interacting causes—or hide actors, choices, and specific harms?

Teacher notes

Learning goals

The word ‘polycrisis’ can direct attention to connections across systems. It can also become vague, make deliberate choices sound like impersonal forces, or flatten different experiences into one global story. Today we test the tool itself and revise project explanations.

  • Reconstruct a careful critique of the polycrisis label without treating it as a settled verdict.
  • Distinguish structural mechanisms, decisions by actors, and differences in perspective.
  • Use peer challenge to revise a project claim, causal map, or source plan.

Your 100-minute lesson

  1. 10 min

    Opening: label or explanation?

    Read ‘Quick classification cards’. Individually classify each statement as a label, mechanism, actor decision, or impact; explain one choice to a partner. Deliverable: four annotations.

  2. 15 min

    Read Tooze’s challenge

    Read ‘Reader: Tooze revisits polycrisis (2025)’ and ‘Argument cards’. Mark his reasons the label can help and can hinder. Deliverable: two supports and two cautions.

  3. 15 min

    Build a fair argument map

    Use ‘Two-sided argument map’ in groups of three. Add one counterpoint and one testable question. Deliverable: completed map with evidence needs.

  4. 5 min

    Break

    Take a five-minute break.

  5. 20 min

    Project challenge circle

    Use ‘Peer challenge roles’ and ‘Project critique protocol’. In groups, one student presents a project claim for two minutes; peers ask mechanism, actor, perspective, and evidence questions. Rotate so at least three projects receive feedback. Deliverable: one feedback form per presenter.

  6. 20 min

    Revise the project

    Use ‘Revision table’ and the common course project rubric/planner to revise the claim, causal map, source plan, or chart interpretation. Deliverable: before/after revision plus one explanation of why it is better.

  7. 10 min

    Individual defence practice

    Use ‘Oral defence prompt cards’. Partner asks one challenge question; presenter answers for 60 seconds with evidence and a limit. Swap roles. Deliverable: one revised oral answer outline.

  8. 5 min

    Exit and homework

    Use ‘Exit and homework slip’ to state one condition under which polycrisis helps your project and one condition under which it would obscure the explanation. Deliverable: individual response.

Materials

Reader: Tooze revisits polycrisis (2025)

Teacher-written summary of Adam Tooze, Chartbook 407 (6 September 2025)

In this essay Tooze asks whether ‘polycrisis’ still describes the general world situation as well as it seemed to in 2022. He says it remains relevant in some specific contexts, while he is less convinced that it names the whole scene. One concern is that broad crisis language can become muddy: it may wave at many forces without identifying their relative weight. In cases where identifiable actors make choices, describing events only as ‘a crisis’ can hide agency and responsibility. Tooze also warns that some specific harms can be obscured by an anonymous general label. At the same time, he does not offer one method that solves every case. Structural forces, networks, and actor-centred explanations can each help, depending on the question, and each has blind spots.

For this course, read the essay as an argument to test, not a final rule. A polycrisis map may help if it reveals a specific interaction that a single-issue account misses: for example, a fuel disruption affecting prices, public budgets, and competition among importers. The map becomes weaker if its arrows are only associations, if it treats a decision as an invisible force, or if it implies that everyone experiences one common crisis in the same way. Ask whose vantage point is used, whose actions are named, and whose experience is absent.

A useful investigation can combine approaches. It may explain a structural constraint, identify the people or institutions making decisions within it, and compare how consequences differ among groups. It should state what evidence supports each link and what remains uncertain. The key question is not whether ‘polycrisis’ is always right or always wrong. It is whether using the term improves this particular explanation, compared with more specific language.

Quick classification cards

  • ‘A drought reduced harvests in a defined region during the stated season.’
  • ‘Higher import costs contributed to pressure on the government budget.’
  • ‘The cabinet chose a subsidy rather than targeted transfers.’
  • ‘Rural households and urban firms experienced the price change differently.’
  • ‘Everything is connected, so no one can be held responsible.’
  • ‘Two events happened together, so one caused the other.’

Classify each as primarily a label, mechanism, actor decision, impact, or unsupported inference. Some statements can fit more than one category; explain your reasoning and identify missing evidence.

Peer challenge roles

  • Presenter: Explain the research question, provisional claim, and one key mechanism in two minutes.
  • Mechanism checker: Ask what evidence supports one arrow and whether another cause is possible.
  • Agency checker: Ask which person, institution, or collective actor made a choice and what constrained it.
  • Perspective checker: Ask who bears costs or is missing from the account.
  • Evidence checker: Ask what source type, date, unit, or contrasting analysis is still needed.

Rotate roles. Critique the explanation, not the person. Do not demand personal disclosures.

Project critique protocol

  1. Presenter gives a two-minute project account without interruption.
  2. Peers take one minute to identify the claim, mechanism, actor, and affected group.
  3. Ask one question each: ‘How do you know?’, ‘Who chose?’, ‘Who experiences this differently?’, ‘What else could explain it?’
  4. Presenter answers, naming one uncertainty rather than pretending certainty.
  5. Peers give one strength and one actionable revision. Presenter records the revision they will make.

Use the common course project rubric/planner. For a class of 24, work in eight groups of three; three 2-minute presentations plus feedback fit the 20-minute circle, so all projects are heard in triads only if presentations are capped at 2 minutes and each feedback round is brief. Otherwise use pairs and teacher circulation.

Peer feedback form

Presenter/project question: …

  • In one sentence, the claim I heard: …
  • Mechanism and evidence that seem strongest: …
  • One arrow or inference to test further: …
  • Actor/decision and constraint to name more clearly: …
  • Whose perspective may be missing: …
  • Alternative explanation or contrasting source to consider: …
  • One revision I recommend (specific and doable): …

Presenter reflection: I will revise … because … / I will keep … because the evidence supports it.

Revision table

Possible revision: narrow the time period; replace ‘caused’ with a more careful verb; add a missing actor; qualify a claim; seek another data series; separate an observed pattern from a causal explanation.

Oral defence prompt cards

  • What is the strongest evidence for your main claim?
  • Which causal link is least certain, and why?
  • What is the best competing explanation?
  • Who benefits and who bears costs under your preferred response?
  • What does the word ‘polycrisis’ add to your explanation?
  • What more precise term could replace it?
  • What new evidence would make you change your mind?

Use a 60-second structure: answer directly; name evidence; explain the mechanism; state one limit.

Exit and homework slip

Exit

  1. When does the polycrisis lens help your project?
  2. When could it hide more than it explains?

Homework (20–25 minutes)

Use your revised paragraph, chart, and source log to add the next 200–300 words or a second evidence paragraph. Check the course project rubric/planner, especially contrasting analysis and source attribution. Bring a complete working draft or clearly marked unfinished sections to lesson 16; use any project time assigned by the teacher to finish.

Language bridge

English pathway. Read the original Chartbook 407 opening through the paragraph ending ‘not so apt’ (the section introducing Tooze’s changed view) and the later passage that begins ‘There are contexts in which crisis talk…’. Read for argument structure rather than every unfamiliar word. Mark one reason for retaining the term, one caution, and one question. Austrian connection: test a project claim against an Austrian institution’s public data or a German-language policy source; explain in English what it adds and what the source’s remit might leave out. Optional extension: read the opening of Chartbook 130 and compare how Tooze framed the term in June 2022 with his September 2025 reassessment.

Useful distinctions: structure = conditions that shape choices; agency = capacity to act; attribution = naming who did what; vantage point = whose experience is used; euphemism = wording that softens or conceals a specific harm.

What to hand in

Individual project revision note, 150–200 words plus one before/after excerpt from the claim, causal map, or source plan. Explain the peer challenge, evidence that informed the revision, and remaining uncertainty. Use the course project rubric/planner. No response is graded for agreeing with Tooze.
