Teacher notes

Learning goals

A country can issue debt in its own currency or in another currency such as US dollars. Exchange-rate changes alter the local-currency cost of foreign-currency debt. You will use a fictional budget model, then connect its mechanism to Sri Lanka's documented crisis without treating the model as a forecast.

  • Calculate how currency depreciation changes the local-currency value of a fixed dollar debt payment.
  • Explain the connection between reserves, imports, debt service, and government choices.
  • Compare debt-management options and defend a response that recognises distributional effects.

Materials

Case reader: A dollar bill and a local budget

Case reader: A dollar bill and a local budget

Governments borrow to pay for services, build infrastructure, meet emergencies, or refinance loans. Debt is a promise to make payments later. The promise may be written in the country's own currency or in a foreign currency. A government that collects most taxes in its own currency but owes dollars needs access to dollars when payments are due. It may earn them through exports, tourism, remittances or borrowing, or use foreign-exchange reserves held by the central bank.

Suppose a government owes US$100 million. If one dollar costs 300 units of local currency, the payment is 30 billion local units. If the local currency loses value and one dollar costs 400 units, the same US$100 million payment costs 40 billion local units. The dollar amount did not change, but its local-currency cost rose by 10 billion, or one third. This simple arithmetic excludes interest, timing and changes in income or prices.

Foreign-currency debt can create pressure when a country has few dollars available, exports or tourism weaken, import bills rise, or lenders stop offering new loans. Paying external debt uses foreign currency that might otherwise pay for fuel, medicine, machinery or food. Stopping payment may preserve foreign currency for a time but can bring default, legal disputes, lost market access and difficult negotiations. Borrowing more can bridge an emergency but may worsen the later burden.

Debt in local currency has different risks. A government may be able to issue its own currency, but creating more money does not create more fuel or medicine. If supply cannot meet demand, prices may rise and the currency may lose value. Local-currency debt can also affect pension funds, banks and savers that hold government bonds. Debt restructuring can change the amount, interest rate, or timing owed, but negotiations distribute losses among creditors and residents and take time.

Sri Lanka's 2022 crisis shows why these connections matter. The IMF's 2023 staff report describes low usable reserves, foreign-exchange shortages, a sharp currency depreciation, suspension of external debt service in April 2022, and debt assessed as unsustainable. The report links the increase in the public-debt ratio to depreciation, deficits and weak economic output. These facts and the IMF analysis do not imply that every country with foreign-currency debt will face the same outcome. The fictional budget isolates one mechanism and asks whose interests choices affect.

Sri Lanka evidence card (dated facts from IMF 2023 staff report)

  • The report says gross international reserves at end-2019 were US$7.6 billion and usable reserves at end-2021 were US$1.6 billion, described as less than one month of imports.
  • The report states that Sri Lanka suspended external debt service on 12 April 2022 and defaulted on international sovereign bonds on 18 May 2022.
  • The report assesses public debt at about 128% of GDP in 2022, attributing the rise in part to currency depreciation, fiscal deficits and negative real GDP growth.
  • The IMF report is an institutional analysis within a lending programme. These facts do not show that foreign-currency debt alone caused the crisis or settle how policy responsibility should be allocated.

Fictional Country L budget and scenarios

Fictional Country L: one-year budget (all figures fictional)

Currency: local units (LU), where 1 LU represents 1 billion units of Country L currency. Expected revenue: 100 LU. Planned spending: essential services 45 LU; fuel and medicine imports 20 LU; local-currency debt payments 10 LU; foreign-currency debt payment of US$100 million (not local-budget units); reserve top-up 10 LU. The planned exchange rate is 300 local-currency units per US$1. At the planned rate, the dollar payment costs 30 LU: US$100 million × 300 local-currency units/US$ = 30 billion local-currency units. Total planned spending is 45 + 20 + 10 + 10 + 30 = 115 LU, leaving a 15 LU gap against revenue even before a shock.

Scenario A — depreciation: Exchange rate changes to 450 local-currency units per US$1. All other spending and revenue stay constant for this calculation. Calculate the dollar payment in LU, total spending and financing gap. This is an accounting exercise, not a forecast.

Scenario B — depreciation plus import-price shock: Exchange rate is 450 local-currency units per US$1. The import bill of 20 LU rises by 25% in local-currency terms (for this exercise only). Revenue remains 100 LU. Calculate the import bill, dollar payment, total spending and financing gap.

Possible response cards (choose two and explain)

  • Use reserves: Pay some dollar bill from reserves. Helps meet payment now; reduces buffer against later imports or shocks.
  • Cut or delay spending: Reduce an item to close some gap. The people relying on that service or investment may bear the cost.
  • Raise revenue: Increase taxes or improve collection. Can improve public finances; design affects households and firms differently and may take time.
  • Seek a bridge loan: Borrow foreign currency. May protect imports now; adds future payment obligations and depends on lender conditions.
  • Negotiate restructuring: Ask creditors to extend maturities, reduce interest, or accept a lower amount. Can reduce near-term outflow; requires negotiation, may impose losses and can take time.
  • Adjust exchange-rate policy: Allow price to change or manage it differently. May reduce reserve use and affect exports/imports; makes imported goods and FX debt costlier in local currency.

Cabinet role cards, rules and portfolio worksheet

Cabinet roles (fictional; share decision-making)

  • Finance minister: Close the financing gap and explain who bears the cost. Do not assume a loan is free.
  • Health and social services minister: Protect essential medicine and basic services. Ask whether cuts shift the crisis onto vulnerable people.
  • Central bank governor: Preserve enough foreign currency for critical imports and explain exchange-rate effects. Distinguish reserves from government revenue.
  • Small exporter and worker representative: Consider how the exchange rate, taxes and credit affect jobs and export earnings; ask who benefits from adjustment.
  • Creditor representative: Ask for a credible repayment plan and fair treatment among creditors; acknowledge that debt restructuring may be necessary if payments are unsustainable.

Cabinet rules

  1. Use Scenario B figures. Each group chooses two response cards; the group must state the first action, who bears its cost, and a risk.
  2. For this exercise, “use reserves” can cover up to 5 LU of the gap, “cut/delay spending” can free up to 6 LU, “raise revenue” can add up to 5 LU next year (not this year), “bridge loan” can cover up to 8 LU now and adds 10 LU of future repayment, “restructure” can reduce this year's dollar payment by up to half but does not erase debt, and exchange-rate adjustment has no automatic gap-reducing score.
  3. These effects are fictional rules for comparison, not estimates of real policy effects. A two-card package may leave a gap. For example, restructuring can reduce this year’s 45 LU FX payment by up to half (22.5 LU), while a bridge loan covers up to 8 LU: together they cover 30.5 LU of Scenario B’s 35 LU gap and leave 4.5 LU unfunded. Groups may not claim the gap is closed unless their stated effects cover it.
  4. Prepare a 90-second cabinet statement: show the arithmetic, name beneficiaries and cost bearers, and state remaining risk. Other groups ask one question.

Individual portfolio entry 2 (220–280 words)

Explain how foreign-currency debt can reduce room to act in a crisis. Include the US$100m conversion calculation, one Sri Lanka evidence-card fact, two response options with their trade-offs, and a judgement about which group might bear costs. Separate fictional exercise results from historical evidence. End with one limit of the evidence.

Worked calculation and source comparison

Worked calculation

US$100 million × 300 local-currency units/US$ = 30,000 million local-currency units = 30 LU (because 1 LU represents 1 billion local-currency units). At 400 units/US$, the same payment is 40 LU. Increase: (40 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = 33.3% (about one third). US$250 million × 400 = 100 LU.

Country L planned spending: 45 + 20 + 10 + 10 + 30 = 115 LU; gap = 115 − 100 = 15 LU. Scenario A: debt service = US$100m × 450 = 45 LU; total = 45 + 20 + 10 + 10 + 45 = 130 LU; gap = 30 LU. Scenario B: import bill = 20 × 1.25 = 25 LU; total = 45 + 25 + 10 + 10 + 45 = 135 LU; gap = 35 LU. The scenarios hold other items fixed and exclude economic feedbacks.

Source comparison prompts

  • IMF Article IV 2022 report: what risks were identified before the acute crisis?
  • IMF 2023 programme report: what later facts and diagnoses were added?
  • World Bank 2022 statement: how does a short contemporaneous account frame the interaction of shocks and structural weakness?
  • For each, distinguish a measured figure, institutional interpretation, and policy recommendation.

Language bridge

English pathway: Work with the English case reader and calculations. Connect to Austria by discussing how Austria's own-currency euro membership differs from a state that earns local currency but owes dollars. The point is not that Austria has no debt risks; identify which currency and institution questions would need checking. For an original-reading extension, use the IMF 2023 staff report's Annex II, section “Current Debt Situation,” paragraphs 1–2, and the paragraph describing debt suspension on 12 April 2022. Extract one number with its unit/date and explain what it cannot prove. Glossary: foreign-currency debt = Fremdwährungsschuld; reserves = Reserven; exchange rate = Wechselkurs; debt restructuring = Umschuldung; room to act = Handlungsspielraum.

Exit ticket

  • How does depreciation change a fixed dollar payment measured in local currency? Show a number.
  • Which response may protect imports now but create a later cost? Explain the trade-off.

After class · up to 25 minutes
