Oil shocks then and now

What can the energy shocks of the 1970s teach us about today's crises—and where does the comparison fail?

Teacher notes

Learning goals

We compare the 1973–74 and 1978–80 oil shocks with Europe's 2021–23 energy crisis. The aim is not to call them identical, but to test which mechanisms recur, who bears the costs, and what historical comparison helps us see. This is portfolio task 3.

  • Compare two energy shocks using evidence and dates.
  • Explain at least two mechanisms linking an energy shock to other problems.
  • Use the course project rubric/planner to write a bounded comparison with uncertainty and limits.

Your 100-minute lesson

  1. 10 min

    Retrieval: draw the chain

    Opening prompt: How can a disruption in one energy system affect people or places that did not cause it? Use ‘Mechanism cards’ to draw one chain from energy supply to household or business effects. Label each arrow with a verb. Deliverable: one annotated chain.

  2. 15 min

    Read the two shock snapshots

    Read ‘Case reader: two oil shocks, one gas shock’ and underline dated events, mechanisms, and interpretations in three different colours. Deliverable: three labelled notes.

  3. 15 min

    Evidence stations

    Use ‘Comparison matrix’ and ‘Evidence cards’. In pairs, complete the first four matrix rows using evidence cards A–D respectively; label each finding with its card, unit, date, and source. Deliverable: four completed matrix rows.

  4. 5 min

    Break

    Take a five-minute break.

  5. 20 min

    Make the comparison map

    In groups of three, build a two-column map with at least three shared mechanisms and two differences. Use ‘Mechanism cards’; mark uncertain or debated links with a question mark. Deliverable: one comparison map.

  6. 15 min

    Portfolio 3: write the comparison

    Individually use ‘Portfolio 3 response frame’ and the course project rubric/planner. State a precise claim, support it with two dated pieces of evidence, explain a mechanism, and name one limit. Deliverable: 180–250 words.

  7. 10 min

    Peer check and revise

    Exchange responses and use ‘Comparison feedback check’. Revise one sentence so it distinguishes evidence from interpretation. Deliverable: revised response plus one feedback note.

  8. 10 min

    Exit: what did history add?

    Answer the two exit questions using ‘Exit and homework slip’. Deliverable: two individual answers.

Materials

Case reader: two oil shocks, one gas shock

Case reader (teacher-written synthesis)

Energy shocks can begin with different events but travel through connected systems. In October 1973, during the Arab-Israeli war, Arab members of OAPEC announced production cuts and an embargo aimed at selected states. The oil market was already under pressure; the embargo and cuts contributed to a sharp rise in oil prices. The US State Department’s contemporary documents show that producers also had political aims and that importers worried about trade balances and supply. In 1978–79, revolution in Iran reduced production: the US Energy Information Administration estimates a fall of about 3.9 million barrels per day in Iranian crude output from 1978 to 1981. Lower supply, panic buying, and market responses contributed to another price shock. These were not simply “oil ran out”: politics, production decisions, expectations, dependence, and limited short-run alternatives all mattered.

Europe’s 2021–23 energy crisis had a different fuel mix and context. The International Energy Agency says markets tightened in 2021 amid rapid post-pandemic recovery, weather, delayed maintenance, and earlier investment choices; Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 sharply worsened the situation. Pipeline gas was central in Europe, while global liquefied natural gas (LNG) trade connected Europe’s search for replacement supplies to Asian importers. The IEA described record gas prices and higher electricity prices in some markets. Governments used support, storage requirements, demand reduction, and new supply arrangements. The shock interacted with household affordability, factory output, inflation, public budgets, and climate decisions.

Some mechanisms recur: concentrated supply, dependence on imported fuels, slow substitution, price transmission, and unequal ability to absorb costs. Important differences remain: the 1970s shocks centred on oil; the recent European shock involved gas, electricity, oil, and a more globally connected LNG market. The institutions, energy efficiency, technology, and political setting also differ. A comparison is useful only when it specifies which mechanism it compares and which evidence could disprove the analogy. Historical similarities do not prove that the same policy will work today.

Mechanism cards

Cut out or assign one card to each pair

  • Import dependence: a country buys much of a fuel from abroad. A disruption can make replacement difficult in the short run.
  • Price transmission: higher fuel costs can raise transport, heating, electricity, and production costs; how much reaches consumers depends on contracts, regulation, taxes, and market design.
  • Unequal exposure: the same price rise can take a larger share of a low-income household’s budget; a firm with energy-intensive production may face different pressure from a service firm.
  • Global competition: when buyers seek replacement fuel, they may compete for cargoes, shifting prices and availability elsewhere.
  • Policy feedback: a subsidy can protect users quickly but raise public costs; conservation can reduce demand but may be harder for some people or industries.
  • Expectations: fear of future shortage may change buying or investment before the physical shortage is fully known.

For each card: connect it to one case, identify who gains or loses, and write one question that would test the connection.

Portfolio 3 response frame

Use the course project rubric/planner. Write 180–250 words answering: What can the 1970s energy shocks help explain about Europe’s 2021–23 energy crisis, and what can they not explain?

  1. My bounded claim is …
  2. Evidence from the 1970s (date, unit/source) shows …
  3. Evidence from 2021–23 shows …
  4. The mechanism connecting them is … because …
  5. A key difference or uncertainty is …
  6. This comparison would be stronger if we also knew …

Comparison feedback check

  • Does the response make a claim more specific than “history repeats”?
  • Are two pieces of evidence dated and attributed?
  • Is at least one mechanism explained in words?
  • Does it distinguish documented evidence from interpretation?
  • Does it identify a meaningful difference, limit, or uncertainty?

Write: “The strongest supported part is …” and “To make the comparison more careful, specify …”

Exit and homework slip

Exit questions

  1. Name one mechanism that recurs across the cases and explain how it works.
  2. What is one important difference that makes a simple analogy misleading?

Homework (20–25 minutes)

Project prework: choose a focused question from lesson 14’s ‘Project route cards’ or propose an equivalent; identify a place/group and time period; log two starting sources from lesson 14’s ‘Source shortlist and fallback investigation’, noting one useful fact/claim and one limitation for each. Bring it to lesson 14.

Language bridge

English pathway. The case reader is teacher-written, so read it first without a dictionary. Then open the original IEA page ‘2022 Energy Crisis’ and read the paragraph beginning ‘Energy markets began to tighten in 2021’ and the paragraph beginning ‘While today’s energy crisis shares some parallels’. In pairs, write a 3-sentence English summary and connect one point to Austrian energy imports or household prices using an Austrian source you locate through Statistik Austria or E-Control. Optional extension: compare the wording of ‘energy security’ and German ‘Versorgungssicherheit’ in one Austrian source. Keep the evidence matrix at the same analytical level; language support does not remove the need to explain mechanisms.

Key terms: supply disruption = Unterbrechung der Versorgung; import dependence = Importabhängigkeit; price transmission = Weitergabe von Kosten/Preisen; exposure = Betroffenheit; embargo = Embargo; uncertainty = Unsicherheit.
