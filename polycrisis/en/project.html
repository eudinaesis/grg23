Use, print and adapt these shared materials.

16 double lessons · Austrian grades 10–12
CourseSyllabusGlossaryToolkitProjectSourcesTeacher guide

Your task: investigate a focused question about how two or more pressures interact. Explain the mechanisms, who is affected, what choices are available, and whether calling the case a polycrisis improves the explanation. A well-supported conclusion may be that the term is partly useful or not useful for your case.

Choose or adapt a question

  • Under what conditions could home renovation reduce energy vulnerability as well as household costs?
  • How can foreign-currency debt constrain a government’s response to a shock?
  • Can battery manufacturing advance climate goals while creating new dependencies?
  • Does the polycrisis framework improve our explanation of Sri Lanka’s 2022 crisis?

Agree a narrow question with your teacher before collecting many sources. Keep the project within one case and a manageable period or policy choice.

Required project elements

  1. A clear question and short explanation of why it matters.
  2. A causal map with explained arrows. Mark which links have evidence, which are uncertain, and at least one alternative explanation or pressure-reducing connection.
  3. Two or three charts that you explain in your own words. State what each chart shows, its data type and date or period, its source, and one limitation. Include the date and data type in the chart caption or nearby text. Do not use a chart as decoration.
  4. At least four sources, including independent evidence and a contrasting argument. Record author or organisation, date, source type, relevant evidence, limits and URL in the evidence log.
  5. A comparison of two possible responses using clear criteria. Include likely beneficiaries, people who may bear costs, practical constraints and possible side effects.
  6. A conclusion that answers your question and judges what the polycrisis framework clarified or concealed.

Length: 1,000–1,200 words for an individual project or a jointly written project of comparable depth, plus map and charts. The source list does not count towards the word total. Each partner submits a brief individual contribution note and completes an individual oral defence. The teacher may set a different accessible format where needed while retaining the same analytical requirements.

Milestones

  1. Session 14 — proposal: submit a focused question, a short reason for choosing it, two possible starting sources and a first sketch of the case boundaries.
  2. Session 14 — evidence plan: list at least four prospective sources, identify which will provide independent evidence and which offers a contrasting argument, and note what evidence you still need.
  3. Session 15 — draft review: bring a draft map, chart choices and a draft claim. Give and receive peer feedback using the course template. Revise at least one part in response to feedback or explain why you did not.
  4. Session 16 — final submission and defence: submit the final work and source log, present the main finding, answer a question about evidence or an alternative explanation, and complete an individual reflection.

Feedback rubric

Use five criteria, each scored from 0 to 3 for feedback: 0 not yet shown; 1 emerging; 2 secure; 3 strong and well supported. Maximum: 15 points. This is a learning rubric, not a mandated conversion to a school grade.

For pair projects, assess the shared work against the rubric and assess each student’s individual understanding through their contribution note, oral defence and reflection.
