Why didn't early dictators seize power?

Roman dictators traditionally depended on the Senate for treasury funding (Polybius, Histories 6.13), were bound to a specific mandate (causa), worked alongside a subordinate magister equitum, and faced intense aristocratic peer pressure and tribunician oversight.

What changed in the 1st century BC?

Professional armies became loyal to generals rather than the Senate. Sulla (82 BC) revived the dormant office with no time limit under the Lex Valeria—yet still resigned the dictatorship in late 81 BC (retiring to private life in 79 BC). Julius Caesar held four numbered dictatorships (49, 48–47, 46–45, and 45–44 BC) before assuming dictator perpetuo in February 44 BC, prompting his assassination and the formal abolition of the office by Mark Antony via the Leges Antoniae.
